# Paper-II Model Answers – Set 2

## 20B.1 Short-Note Answers (5-mark style)
Q: Write a short note on creep.
Creep is slow, time-dependent permanent deformation under constant load, significant above about 0.4 Tm (absolute melting temperature). A creep curve (strain vs time at constant load and temperature) shows instantaneous elastic strain, then primary creep (decreasing rate due to strain hardening), secondary or steady-state creep (constant minimum rate, a balance between hardening and recovery – used for design), and tertiary creep (accelerating rate from necking and void formation, ending in rupture). Creep matters in turbine blades, boiler tubes, steam pipes, IC engine valves and lead pipes (lead creeps at room temperature). Creep-resistant materials have high melting points, coarse grains (fewer grain boundaries), solid-solution and precipitate strengthening; single-crystal nickel superalloys are used for turbine blades.
Q: Write a short note on fatigue failure.
Fatigue is failure under repeated or fluctuating stresses well below the ultimate (often below yield) strength after many cycles. It has three stages: crack initiation at stress raisers (surface scratches, notches, inclusions), crack propagation (beach marks or striations on the fracture surface), and sudden final fracture of the reduced section (rough, granular zone). It causes most failures of machine parts such as shafts, springs, gears and aircraft components. The S–N curve and endurance limit describe resistance. Improve fatigue life by reducing stress concentration (fillets), improving surface finish, inducing compressive residual stress (shot peening, surface rolling, nitriding), avoiding corrosion and choosing suitable materials.
Q: Write a short note on cavitation and NPSH.
Cavitation itself is explained in the first Paper-II answers chapter. NPSH (net positive suction head) is the absolute pressure head at the pump suction above the vapour pressure head: NPSH_A = p_atm/ρg − h_s − h_fs − p_v/ρg. NPSH_A must exceed NPSH_R (given by the manufacturer) to avoid cavitation. Increase NPSH_A by lowering the pump (reducing suction lift), using a larger suction pipe with fewer fittings (less friction), cooling the liquid (lower vapour pressure) or pressurising the suction tank.
Q: Write a short note on knocking in SI engines.
Knocking (detonation) is spontaneous auto-ignition of the last portion of unburnt charge (end gas) before the normal flame front reaches it. It happens because the end gas is compressed and heated by the expanding burnt gases ahead of the flame. The sudden pressure rise sets up pressure waves that hit the cylinder walls, producing a metallic pinging sound, loss of power, overheating, and damage to pistons, rings and spark plug electrodes. It is promoted by high CR, high intake temperature and pressure, advanced spark timing, low-octane fuel, large bore, lean mixtures and low speed. It is controlled by using high-octane fuel or anti-knock additives, retarding the spark (knock sensors), lowering CR, compact combustion chambers with central spark plugs, more turbulence, cooling the end-gas region and EGR.
Q: Write a short note on the boundary layer and its separation.
When a real fluid flows past a surface, the no-slip condition makes velocity zero at the wall. A thin layer forms in which velocity rises to the free-stream value U and viscous effects are important: this is the boundary layer. Its thickness δ (where u = 0.99U) grows downstream. It starts laminar (δ ∝ √x) and becomes turbulent beyond Re_x ≈ 5 × 10⁵ on a flat plate, with a thin laminar sub-layer remaining near the wall. Under an adverse pressure gradient (pressure increasing downstream, as at the rear of a cylinder or in a diffuser), the slow fluid near the wall loses momentum and stops; where (∂u/∂y) at the wall = 0, the flow separates and reverse flow forms a wake. This greatly raises pressure drag. Separation is controlled by streamlining, suction or blowing of the boundary layer, slotted wings, vortex generators, rotating cylinders and turbulent tripping (golf ball dimples).
Q: Write a short note on hydraulic jump.
A hydraulic jump is the sudden rise of the water surface when supercritical flow (Fr > 1, shallow and fast) changes to subcritical flow (Fr < 1, deep and slow) in an open channel, for example at the foot of a spillway or below a sluice gate. It involves violent turbulence and rollers. Much energy is dissipated, which is useful for protecting the downstream channel bed from scour. It also raises the water level for irrigation, mixes chemicals in water treatment and aerates water. Sequent depths: y₂/y₁ = ½[−1 + √(1 + 8Fr₁²)]. Energy loss = (y₂ − y₁)³/(4y₁y₂).
Q: Write a short note on psychrometric chart.
A psychrometric chart is a graphical representation of the properties of moist air at a given barometric pressure (usually 1.01325 bar). Dry bulb temperature is on the horizontal axis and specific humidity (with vapour pressure) on the vertical axis. The saturation curve (100% RH) is the upper boundary, with relative humidity curves below it. Wet bulb temperature lines slope down to the right and nearly coincide with constant-enthalpy lines (enthalpy scale on the diagonal). Specific volume lines are steeper. Dew point is read horizontally at the saturation curve. Two independent properties locate a state, and the chart shows AC processes as lines: sensible heating and cooling horizontal, humidification and dehumidification vertical, evaporative cooling along WBT lines, and mixing along a straight line between states.
Q: Write a short note on CNC machines.
A CNC (computer numerical control) machine tool is controlled by a dedicated microcomputer that reads a part program (G and M codes) and drives the axes with servo or stepper motors through ball screws, using feedback from encoders or linear scales in closed-loop systems. Advantages: high accuracy and repeatability, complex contours by interpolation, reduced set-up and lead time, flexibility (just change the program), less operator skill needed, unmanned operation, easy integration with CAD/CAM. Limitations: high initial and maintenance cost and the need for trained programmers. Developments: machining centres with automatic tool changers and pallet changers, DNC networks, adaptive control, and multi-axis (5-axis) machines.
Q: Write a short note on Just-in-Time (JIT).
JIT, developed at Toyota (Taiichi Ohno), produces and delivers only what is needed, when it is needed and in the quantity needed. It aims at eliminating the seven wastes (overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, defects). Tools: pull system with Kanban cards, small lot sizes and set-up reduction (SMED), level (heijunka) scheduling, a few reliable suppliers close to the plant, total productive maintenance, quality at the source (jidoka, poka-yoke), multi-skilled workers and continuous improvement (kaizen). Benefits: low inventory and floor space, shorter lead times, better quality and exposure of hidden problems. Risks: vulnerability to supply disruptions and the need for stable demand.

## 20B.2 Explain-with-Diagram Answers
Q: Explain the working of a two-stroke petrol engine and compare it with a four-stroke engine.
In a crankcase-scavenged two-stroke engine, the piston itself acts as the valve through ports. Upward stroke: the piston compresses the charge above it, and its rising skirt uncovers the inlet port so a fresh mixture is drawn into the sealed crankcase by the partial vacuum. Near TDC the spark ignites the charge. Downward stroke: the burning gases push the piston down (power), and the mixture in the crankcase is compressed slightly. The exhaust port is uncovered first (blow-down), then the transfer port, through which compressed crankcase charge flows into the cylinder and pushes out the exhaust (scavenging, aided by the deflector on the piston crown). So one power stroke occurs every revolution. Compared with four-stroke: simpler, lighter, cheaper, no valve gear, more uniform torque and a lighter flywheel, and theoretically twice the power for the same size. But it has lower thermal and volumetric efficiency, higher fuel consumption due to short-circuiting of fresh charge, higher HC emissions, higher lubricating oil consumption (petroil) and more wear and noise. So it suits mopeds, chainsaws and large marine diesels (uniflow).
Q: Explain the working of a Pelton wheel with a velocity triangle and derive the condition for maximum efficiency.
Water from a high-head reservoir is carried by the penstock to a nozzle with a spear valve, forming a high-velocity jet V₁ = Cv√(2gH). The jet strikes the splitter of double-cup buckets on the wheel periphery (speed u), is divided into two halves, and is deflected back through about 165°. Inlet triangle: V₁ and u are collinear, so relative velocity Vr₁ = V₁ − u and Vw₁ = V₁. Outlet: Vr₂ = kVr₁ at bucket angle φ to the reverse direction, so Vw₂ = Vr₂cosφ − u (opposite direction). Work per kg = (Vw₁ + Vw₂)u = (V₁ − u)(1 + k cosφ)u. Hydraulic efficiency ηh = 2(V₁ − u)(1 + k cosφ)u/V₁². Setting dηh/du = 0 gives V₁ − 2u = 0, so u = V₁/2, and ηh,max = (1 + k cosφ)/2. With k = 1 and φ = 0 this becomes 100% in theory. In practice u ≈ 0.46V₁ and the overall efficiency is about 85–90%.
Q: Explain the working of a steam power plant with a layout and state the function of each component.
Coal from the storage yard is crushed and pulverised and burnt in the boiler furnace with air preheated in the air preheater. The forced-draught fan supplies air; the induced-draught fan and chimney remove flue gases after the ESP removes fly ash. Water from the feed pump passes through the economiser (heated by flue gas), then the boiler drum and water walls (evaporation), and the superheater, producing high-pressure superheated steam. Steam expands in the HP turbine, returns to the reheater, then expands through the IP and LP turbines, which drive the alternator. Exhaust steam condenses in a surface condenser cooled by water from the cooling tower. Condensate is pumped through LP feed heaters, the deaerator (removes dissolved O₂ and CO₂) and HP feed heaters (bled steam, regeneration) back to the boiler. Ash handling and coal handling plants, water treatment (to prevent scaling), and switchyard complete the plant. Overall efficiency is about 33–40%.
Q: Describe the shaping machine quick-return mechanism (crank and slotted lever) with a sketch.
A bull gear driven by the motor carries a crank pin with a sliding block that fits in the slot of a slotted lever. The lever pivots at its lower end and its upper end connects to the ram, which carries the tool. As the bull gear rotates uniformly, the block slides in the slot and oscillates the lever, which reciprocates the ram. Extreme positions occur when the crank is perpendicular to the slotted lever. The crank turns through the larger angle (360° − α) during the cutting stroke and the smaller angle α during the return stroke, so the return is quicker (ratio = (360° − α)/α, typically about 3:2). Stroke length is changed by altering the crank radius (the crank pin position on the bull gear), and stroke position by moving the ram clamp. Idle return time is reduced, which increases productivity.
Q: Explain the iron–carbon diagram based heat treatment of hardening and tempering with its objectives.
Hardening: a hypoeutectoid steel is heated 30–50 °C above A₃ (above A₁ for hypereutectoid), held to form homogeneous austenite, and quenched in water, oil or brine faster than the critical cooling rate. Austenite transforms without diffusion into martensite, a supersaturated BCT solution of carbon in iron. It is very hard and strong but brittle and highly stressed, sometimes with retained austenite. Objectives: high hardness and wear resistance for tools, dies, gears and bearings. Tempering: the hardened steel is reheated below A₁ and cooled. Martensite decomposes into tempered martensite (fine carbides in ferrite). Hardness drops slightly, while toughness and ductility rise a lot and internal stresses are relieved. Low-temperature tempering (150–250 °C) is for cutting tools, medium (350–450 °C) for springs, high (500–650 °C, giving sorbite) for shafts, axles and connecting rods. Hardening plus high-temperature tempering is called hardening and tempering (H&T or Q&T).
Q: Explain the principle and working of electric discharge machining.
EDM removes metal by a rapid series of controlled electric sparks between a shaped tool electrode (usually the cathode – copper, brass or graphite) and the workpiece (anode), both submerged in a dielectric fluid (kerosene, transformer oil or deionised water). A pulsed DC supply (RC relaxation circuit or transistorised pulse generator) charges the gap (0.01–0.5 mm). When the voltage exceeds the dielectric's breakdown strength, a plasma channel forms, with local temperatures of 8000–12000 °C, which melts and vaporises a tiny crater on both surfaces. The dielectric quenches and flushes away the debris and deionises the gap for the next spark. A servo system keeps the gap constant as the tool feeds in. MRR depends on spark energy (½CV²) and frequency; finish improves with lower energy and higher frequency. Applications: dies, moulds, complex cavities, small holes in hard conductive materials (hardened steel, carbides). Limitations: the work must be conductive, the tool wears, MRR is slow, and a recast layer and HAZ form.

## 20B.3 Compare-and-Contrast Answers
Q: Compare impulse and reaction turbines (steam/water).
Impulse: all pressure energy is converted to kinetic energy in nozzles. Moving blades receive a high-velocity jet at constant pressure and only change its direction. Blades are symmetrical. Partial admission is possible. Casing has no hydraulic function. Examples: De Laval and Curtis steam turbines, Pelton wheel. Reaction: pressure drops in both fixed and moving blades. The fluid accelerates through moving blades too, producing a reaction force. Blades are aerofoil-shaped and form nozzles. Full admission is needed, and leakage across blade tips matters (balance piston for axial thrust). In water turbines the runner is full of water and a draft tube is used. Examples: Parsons steam turbine, Francis and Kaplan. Reaction turbines need more stages but are more efficient at their design point.
Q: Compare casting, forging and machining as manufacturing routes for a crankshaft.
Casting (nodular CI): low cost, near-net shape, complex shapes, good damping. But it has lower strength and fatigue resistance and possible porosity. Used for low-to-medium duty cars. Forging (medium-carbon or alloy steel, closed die): continuous grain flow following the contour gives high strength, toughness and fatigue life. The die cost is high and it needs finish machining. Used for high-performance and diesel engines. Machining from solid bar: no tooling cost and flexible, but the grain flow is cut, so fatigue strength is lower, and there is much material waste and long machining time. Used only for prototypes and single pieces. Forged crankshafts are preferred for heavy duty and cast SG iron for economy.
Q: Compare annealing and normalising.
Both heat the steel into the austenite range (annealing about 30–50 °C above A₃/A₁; normalising about 40–50 °C above A₃/A_cm). Annealing cools slowly in the furnace, giving coarse pearlite, lowest hardness, maximum ductility and machinability, and full stress relief. It takes longer and costs more. Normalising cools in still air, giving fine pearlite and finer grain, higher strength and hardness, and a more uniform structure. It is quicker and cheaper and is used after forging or casting to refine grains, often as a preliminary to hardening. Hypereutectoid steels are not fully annealed above A_cm because a brittle cementite network would form; they are spheroidised instead.
