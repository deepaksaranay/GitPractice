# Paper-II Style Model Answers
Paper-II rewards structured answers: definition → sketch (describe it in words if you can't draw) → principle/derivation → applications → advantages and limitations. Each model answer below is written that way. Learn the structure, not the sentences.

## 20.1 Strength of Materials & Mechanics
Q: Derive the expression for the elongation of a uniformly tapering circular bar under axial load P.
Let the diameter vary linearly from d₁ to d₂ over length L. At distance x from the larger end, dₓ = d₁ − kx, with k = (d₁ − d₂)/L. The elongation of an elemental length dx is dδ = P dx / (E·(π/4)dₓ²). Integrate from 0 to L: δ = (4P/πE)∫dx/(d₁ − kx)² = (4P/πEk)[1/(d₁ − kx)] from 0 to L = (4P/πEk)(1/d₂ − 1/d₁) = (4P/πEk)·(d₁ − d₂)/(d₁d₂). Substitute k: δ = 4PL/(πEd₁d₂). Check: when d₁ = d₂ = d, δ = PL/(AE), as expected.
Q: Derive the expression for strain energy stored in a body due to an impact load falling from height h.
Let a load P fall through h onto a collar at the end of a bar of length L and area A, producing extension δ and instantaneous stress σ. Work done by the load = P(h + δ). Strain energy stored = (σ²/2E)AL. With δ = σL/E: P(h + σL/E) = σ²AL/(2E). This is a quadratic in σ: (AL/2E)σ² − (PL/E)σ − Ph = 0. Solving: σ = (P/A)[1 + √(1 + 2AEh/(PL))]. Special cases: h = 0 (sudden load) gives σ = 2P/A. If h ≫ δ, then σ ≈ √(2EPh/(AL)).
Q: Explain the assumptions of Euler's theory of columns and its limitations.
Assumptions: the column is initially perfectly straight and the load is exactly axial; the material is homogeneous, isotropic and obeys Hooke's law; the cross-section is uniform; the self-weight is ignored; the column fails only by buckling; and the length is large compared with the lateral dimensions (direct stress is negligible). Limitations: Euler's formula gives crippling stress π²E/λ², which exceeds the crushing stress for short columns, so it is valid only when λ exceeds the limiting slenderness ratio (≈ 80 for mild steel). Real columns have initial crookedness and load eccentricity, so they fail below Euler's load. The Rankine–Gordon formula covers both short and long columns empirically.
Q: Draw the SFD and BMD for a simply supported beam carrying a UDL w over span L and locate the maximum BM.
Reactions: R_A = R_B = wL/2. At distance x from A: SF = wL/2 − wx, a straight line from +wL/2 to −wL/2 that is zero at x = L/2. BM = (wL/2)x − wx²/2, a parabola that is zero at both supports. Since dM/dx = SF = 0 at x = L/2, the maximum BM there is wL/2·L/2 − w(L/2)²/2 = wL²/8 (sagging). There is no point of contraflexure.
Q: Explain the principle of virtual work / work–energy principle with an application.
Work–energy principle: the net work done by all forces on a body equals its change in kinetic energy, W_net = ½m(v₂² − v₁²). It is derived by integrating Newton's second law, F = m v dv/ds, along the path. Application: finding the stopping distance of a vehicle on a rough road. Friction work μmg·s = ½mv², so s = v²/(2μg). Mass does not affect it. Advantages: it avoids computing acceleration, and it handles variable forces when the work is known.

## 20.2 Theory of Machines & Machine Design
Q: Derive the ratio of belt tensions for a flat belt, T₁/T₂ = e^(μθ).
Take a belt element subtending angle δθ at the pulley centre, with tensions T and T + δT at its ends. Normal reaction R; friction μR acts along the belt. Radial equilibrium: R = (T + δT)sin(δθ/2) + T sin(δθ/2) ≈ T δθ (neglecting second-order terms). Tangential equilibrium: μR = (T + δT)cos(δθ/2) − T cos(δθ/2) ≈ δT. Hence δT/T = μ δθ. Integrating from T₂ to T₁ over the arc of contact θ: ln(T₁/T₂) = μθ, so T₁/T₂ = e^(μθ). For V-belts the normal reaction is increased by the wedge action (2R sinβ), giving e^(μθ cosec β).
Q: What is interference in involute gears? State the methods to avoid it.
The involute profile exists only outside the base circle. If the addendum of the mating gear extends beyond the interference point (the tangency point of the line of action with the base circle), its tip contacts the non-involute flank below the base circle. This is interference: the tip digs into the flank, removes material (undercutting) and weakens the tooth. Avoid it by: (1) increasing the number of teeth on the pinion (≥ 18 for 20°, 32 for 14.5°); (2) increasing the pressure angle; (3) using stub teeth with reduced addendum; (4) profile shifting (positive correction on the pinion); (5) undercutting the flank during generation (but this weakens the tooth).
Q: Explain the working of a centrifugal governor (Porter) and derive the expression for its height.
A Porter governor has two balls of mass m on arms pivoted at the spindle, with lower links joined to a central sleeve of mass M that slides on the spindle. When speed rises, centrifugal force throws the balls outward and lifts the sleeve. The sleeve, through a lever, reduces the fuel or steam supply, and the reverse happens when speed falls. For equal arm and link lengths, take moments about the instantaneous centre of the lower link (ignoring friction): mω²r·h = mg·r + (M/2)g·2r. This gives h = (m + M)g/(mω²), i.e., h = (m + M)/m × 895/N² (m, N in rpm). A Watt governor is the special case M = 0: h = 895/N².
Q: Explain why rolling contact bearings are preferred in some applications and sliding bearings in others.
Rolling bearings (ball, roller) have low starting friction, need little lubrication and maintenance, take combined loads, are standardised and easily replaced, and keep accurate shaft alignment. They are preferred in automobiles, machine tool spindles and electric motors. However, they are noisy at high speed, have finite fatigue life, are sensitive to shock and need more radial space. Sliding (journal) bearings with a hydrodynamic film run silently, damp vibration, carry very heavy and shock loads, have practically unlimited life with proper lubrication, and are compact radially. They suit turbines, large generators, IC engine crankshafts and connecting rods. Their drawbacks are high starting friction and the need for continuous lubrication.
Q: Explain the design procedure for a cotter joint (socket and spigot).
Failure modes considered, with load P: (1) tension in the rod, d = √(4P/πσt); (2) tension across the slot of the spigot, P = [(π/4)d₂² − d₂t]σt; (3) tension across the slot of the socket, P = [(π/4)(d₁² − d₂²) − (d₁ − d₂)t]σt; (4) shear of the cotter, P = 2bt·τ; (5) crushing of spigot on cotter, P = d₂tσc; (6) crushing of socket collar, P = (d₄ − d₂)tσc; (7) shear of the spigot end, P = 2a d₂ τ; (8) shear of the socket end, P = 2(d₄ − d₂)cτ; (9) bending of the cotter. Empirical proportions are first assumed: t ≈ 0.31d, d₂ ≈ 1.21d, d₁ ≈ 1.75d. Each mode is then checked and the dimensions are increased where a stress exceeds the allowable value.

## 20.3 Thermal Engineering
Q: Derive the air-standard efficiency of the Otto cycle.
Processes: 1–2 isentropic compression; 2–3 heat addition at constant volume, Q₁ = mcv(T₃ − T₂); 3–4 isentropic expansion; 4–1 heat rejection at constant volume, Q₂ = mcv(T₄ − T₁). Efficiency η = 1 − Q₂/Q₁ = 1 − (T₄ − T₁)/(T₃ − T₂). For isentropic processes with compression ratio r = V₁/V₂ = V₄/V₃: T₂/T₁ = T₃/T₄ = r^(γ−1). Hence T₃ − T₂ = r^(γ−1)(T₄ − T₁), and η = 1 − 1/r^(γ−1). Efficiency rises with r but is limited by knock in SI engines. It does not depend on heat added.
Q: Explain the working of a four-stroke diesel engine with a valve timing diagram.
Suction: the inlet valve opens (about 10–20° before TDC) and the piston moves down drawing in air only; the valve closes 25–50° after BDC to use the inertia ram effect. Compression: both valves closed; air is compressed (CR 14–22) to 30–45 bar and about 600 °C. Power: fuel is injected starting 10–15° before TDC, self-ignites after a short delay, and burns roughly at constant pressure; the gases expand and push the piston down. Exhaust: the exhaust valve opens 30–50° before BDC (blow-down) and the piston expels gases, closing 10–15° after TDC. Near TDC both valves are open (valve overlap) to scavenge residual gases. One power stroke occurs every two crank revolutions, so a flywheel is needed.
Q: Explain the effects of superheating, condenser pressure and boiler pressure on the Rankine cycle using a T–s diagram.
Superheating raises the mean temperature of heat addition, increasing efficiency and work output. It also moves the expansion line to the right, so exhaust steam is drier and turbine blades erode less. Lowering condenser pressure lowers the mean temperature of heat rejection, which increases work and efficiency, but exhaust moisture rises and air leakage into the condenser increases. Raising boiler pressure at fixed maximum temperature raises the mean temperature of heat addition (higher η), but the expansion line shifts left and exhaust dryness drops. So high pressure is combined with reheating to keep exhaust quality above about 0.88.
Q: Explain the working of a vapour absorption refrigeration system (aqua-ammonia) and compare it with VCR.
Low-pressure ammonia vapour from the evaporator is absorbed by weak aqua-ammonia solution in the absorber; heat of absorption is removed by cooling water. A small pump raises the strong solution to condenser pressure through a heat exchanger into the generator. There heat (steam, waste heat or solar) drives off ammonia vapour. An analyser and rectifier remove water vapour from it. Pure ammonia condenses in the condenser, is throttled and evaporates in the evaporator to produce cooling. The weak solution returns to the absorber through a pressure-reducing valve. Comparison: VAR uses heat energy and a tiny pump in place of a compressor, so it has few moving parts, runs quietly and suits waste heat and large capacities. Its COP (0.5–0.7) is lower than VCR (2–5), and it is bulkier with a higher initial cost.
Q: Explain modes of heat transfer and derive the heat flow equation through a composite plane wall.
Conduction is molecular energy transfer in a medium without bulk motion (Fourier's law). Convection is energy transfer between a surface and a moving fluid (Newton's law of cooling). Radiation is electromagnetic emission needing no medium (Stefan–Boltzmann law). For a composite wall of layers L₁, L₂, L₃ with conductivities k₁, k₂, k₃ and convection h_i, h_o on the two faces, the same heat Q flows through each resistance in series. Q = (T_i − T_o) / [1/(h_iA) + L₁/(k₁A) + L₂/(k₂A) + L₃/(k₃A) + 1/(h_oA)], and the overall coefficient is U = 1/[1/h_i + ΣL/k + 1/h_o]. Interface temperatures follow from Q multiplied by each partial resistance.

## 20.4 Fluid Mechanics & Machines
Q: Derive Hagen–Poiseuille's equation for laminar flow in a circular pipe.
Take a coaxial fluid cylinder of radius r and length L. Pressure force (−dp/dx)πr²L balances the shear force τ·2πrL, so τ = (−dp/dx)r/2: shear varies linearly from zero at the axis to maximum at the wall. With τ = −μ du/dr, integrating and using u = 0 at r = R gives u = (−dp/dx)(R² − r²)/(4μ), a parabolic profile with u_max at the centre. Discharge Q = ∫u·2πr dr = (π/8μ)(−dp/dx)R⁴, so mean velocity V = u_max/2. Rearranging: Δp = 32μVL/D², and head loss h_f = 32μVL/(ρgD²), which corresponds to f = 64/Re.
Q: Define specific speed of a turbine, derive its expression and explain its significance.
Specific speed is the speed of a geometrically similar turbine that produces unit power under unit head. For similar turbines, u ∝ √H and u ∝ DN, so D ∝ √H/N. Discharge Q ∝ area × velocity ∝ D²√H. Power P ∝ QH ∝ D²H^(3/2) ∝ H^(5/2)/N². For the specific turbine (P = 1, H = 1, N = Ns), the proportionality constant gives P = (Ns²/N²)H^(5/2), so Ns = N√P/H^(5/4). Significance: it classifies turbines (Pelton low, Francis medium, Kaplan high), predicts performance of prototypes from models, and guides selection for a given head and power.
Q: Explain cavitation in hydraulic machines – causes, effects and remedies.
Cavitation is the formation of vapour bubbles where local static pressure falls below the liquid's vapour pressure, and their violent collapse when they reach higher-pressure zones. Causes: high suction lift, high speed, high water temperature, low atmospheric pressure (altitude), and poor blade design. It occurs at the runner outlet of reaction turbines and the impeller eye of pumps. Effects: pitting and erosion of metal surfaces (very high local pressures on collapse), noise and vibration, and a sudden drop in head, discharge and efficiency. Remedies: keep pressure above vapour pressure by limiting suction head and keeping NPSH available above NPSH required; set turbines lower (Thoma's σ above critical); use cavitation-resistant materials (stainless steel, bronze) and polished surfaces; avoid overloading and excess speed.
Q: Describe the working of a centrifugal pump and explain the importance of priming.
The impeller, keyed to a motor-driven shaft, rotates inside a volute casing. Liquid enters the eye axially, is turned radially outward by backward-curved vanes, and gains kinetic and pressure energy through centrifugal (forced-vortex) action. The volute's gradually increasing area converts much of the velocity head into pressure before the liquid reaches the delivery pipe. Priming fills the suction pipe, casing and impeller with liquid before starting. Head developed is H = Vw₂u₂/g regardless of fluid, so pressure rise ∝ ρ. With air the pressure developed is about 1/800 of that with water, which cannot lift water up the suction pipe. A foot valve (non-return) keeps the pump primed; self-priming designs and jet-assisted priming are alternatives.

## 20.5 Production & Industrial Engineering
Q: Explain the various allowances provided on patterns with reasons.
(1) Shrinkage allowance: the pattern is made oversize to compensate for liquid, solidification and solid contraction (CI ≈ 1%, steel ≈ 2%). Contraction rulers are used. (2) Machining allowance: extra metal on surfaces to be machined, larger for rough castings, CI, big parts and internal surfaces. (3) Draft allowance: a 1–3° taper on vertical faces so the pattern can be withdrawn without damaging the mould; internal surfaces get more. (4) Distortion allowance: the pattern is deliberately distorted in the opposite sense for long, thin or U-shaped castings that warp on cooling. (5) Shake allowance: rapping enlarges the cavity, so the pattern is made slightly smaller. It is the only negative allowance.
Q: Compare TIG and MIG welding.
TIG (GTAW) uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode with separate filler rod if needed and an inert gas shield (argon/helium). It gives high-quality, spatter-free welds on thin sheets and non-ferrous metals (Al, Mg, stainless). AC is used for aluminium for its cleaning action and DCSP for steels. Deposition rate is low and it needs skilled operators. MIG (GMAW) uses a continuously fed consumable bare wire that is both electrode and filler, with inert or CO₂ shielding (MAG) and DCRP with a constant-voltage source. It is faster, has higher deposition, is easy to automate and suits thicker sections and production welding. It produces some spatter and the equipment is costlier.
Q: Explain Taylor's tool life equation and factors affecting tool life.
Taylor's experiments showed that cutting speed and tool life follow VTⁿ = C. Here n depends mainly on tool material (≈ 0.1–0.15 for HSS, 0.2–0.5 for carbides, 0.5–0.7 for ceramics), and C is the speed giving 1 min tool life. On log–log axes it is a straight line of slope −1/n. The extended form VTⁿf^a d^b = C includes feed and depth of cut. Factors: cutting speed (strongest influence), feed, depth of cut, tool material and geometry (rake, clearance, nose radius), work material (hardness, microstructure, inclusions), cutting fluid, rigidity of machine and set-up, and type of cut (continuous or interrupted).
Q: Derive the EOQ formula and state its assumptions.
Assumptions: constant and known annual demand D; instantaneous replenishment; constant lead time; no shortages; constant ordering cost Co per order and holding cost Ch per unit per year; no quantity discounts. With order quantity Q: number of orders = D/Q, so annual ordering cost = (D/Q)Co. Average inventory = Q/2, so annual holding cost = (Q/2)Ch. Total cost TC = (D/Q)Co + (Q/2)Ch. Setting dTC/dQ = −DCo/Q² + Ch/2 = 0 gives Q* = √(2DCo/Ch). At Q*, ordering cost equals holding cost, and TC_min = √(2DCoCh).
Q: Compare PERT and CPM.
CPM (Critical Path Method, DuPont, 1957) is deterministic with one time estimate per activity. It is activity-oriented, suits repetitive projects with known durations (construction, maintenance) and emphasises time–cost trade-off (crashing). PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique, US Navy Polaris, 1958) is probabilistic with three estimates (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic) fitted to a β-distribution, te = (to + 4tm + tp)/6. It is event-oriented, suits R&D and first-of-a-kind projects and gives the probability of meeting a target date using the normal distribution along the critical path. Both use network diagrams, forward and backward passes, floats and the critical path (zero total float).
