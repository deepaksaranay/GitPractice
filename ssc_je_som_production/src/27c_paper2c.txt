# Paper-II Model Answers – Set 3
Further descriptive answers covering topics that frequently appear in conventional papers.

## P5. Mechanics and SOM
Q: State the laws of dry friction and explain the angle of friction and angle of repose.
The laws of static (Coulomb) friction are: (1) the frictional force always acts opposite to the direction in which the body tends to move; (2) up to the limiting value, the friction force is exactly equal to the applied force and the body stays at rest (friction is self-adjusting); (3) the limiting friction bears a constant ratio to the normal reaction, F = μN; (4) limiting friction is independent of the apparent area of contact; (5) it depends on the nature and condition of the surfaces. For kinetic friction, the friction force is slightly less than limiting friction, roughly constant for moderate speeds, and the ratio F_k/N = μ_k is less than μ_s. The angle of friction φ is the angle that the resultant of the limiting friction force and normal reaction makes with the normal: tanφ = F/N = μ. If a body is placed on an inclined plane and the inclination is slowly increased, the body is on the point of sliding when the component of weight along the plane W sinα equals the limiting friction μW cosα, i.e. tanα = μ. This limiting angle α is the angle of repose, and it equals the angle of friction. Rotating the resultant reaction about the normal generates the cone of friction of semi-vertex angle φ; a body stays at rest as long as the resultant force lies within the cone.
Q: Explain the method of joints and the method of sections for analysing trusses.
A truss is analysed on the assumptions that members are joined by frictionless pins, loads act only at joints and self-weight is neglected, so each member is a two-force member in pure tension or compression. In the method of joints, the support reactions are first found from the equilibrium of the whole truss. Then a joint at which not more than two unknown member forces meet is isolated; since the forces are concurrent, two equations ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 give the two unknowns. Proceeding from joint to joint, all member forces are found. Forces directed away from a joint indicate tension, towards it compression. It is systematic but forces in a central member may require analysing many joints first, and errors accumulate. In the method of sections, an imaginary section is passed through the truss cutting not more than three members whose forces are unknown and which are not all concurrent or parallel. One part of the truss is taken as a free body; three equations ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0 and ΣM = 0 are applied, taking moments about the point where two unknown forces intersect to find the third directly. It is quick for finding forces in a few particular members. Zero-force members can be identified first by inspection to simplify both methods.
Q: Explain the strain energy method (Castigliano's theorem) for finding deflections.
Castigliano's first theorem states that, for a linearly elastic structure, the partial derivative of the total strain energy with respect to an external load equals the deflection of the point of application of that load in the direction of the load: δ = ∂U/∂P. Similarly, the partial derivative with respect to an applied couple gives the rotation: θ = ∂U/∂M. For a beam, U = ∫M²dx/(2EI) (shear energy usually neglected), so δ = ∫(M/EI)(∂M/∂P)dx. To find the deflection at a point where no load acts, a fictitious load Q is applied there, the expression is differentiated with respect to Q, and Q is then set to zero. For example, for a cantilever with end load W, M = −Wx and ∂M/∂W = −x, so δ = (1/EI)∫₀ᴸ Wx² dx = WL³/(3EI). The method is particularly useful for curved bars, rings, frames, springs and beams of varying cross-section, and its companion theorem of least work (∂U/∂R = 0 for a redundant reaction) is used to analyse statically indeterminate structures such as propped cantilevers and fixed beams.

## P6. Production Engineering
Q: Explain the working of a cupola furnace.
The cupola is a vertical cylindrical steel shell lined with refractory bricks, supported on legs, with a drop-bottom door at its base. Air from a blower enters a wind box and passes into the furnace through tuyeres located a little above the bottom. Before the blast, a coke bed is laid and ignited up to a height above the tuyeres; then alternate layers of charge – pig iron, foundry return scrap and steel scrap, coke as fuel (metal-to-coke ratio about 8–10:1) and limestone as flux – are charged from the charging door near the top. The furnace has zones: the well or crucible (below the tuyeres) where molten metal collects; the combustion (oxidising) zone just above the tuyeres where coke burns to CO₂, releasing heat and reaching about 1500–1800 °C; the reducing zone where CO₂ reacts with hot coke to form CO, protecting the metal from oxidation; the melting zone where iron melts and picks up carbon; the preheating zone where the descending charge is heated by rising gases; and the stack through which gases leave. Molten iron is tapped from the tap hole at intervals, and slag (formed by limestone combining with ash, sand and oxides) is removed from the slag hole at a higher level. Cupolas are simple, cheap and continuous for melting grey cast iron, though temperature and composition control are less precise than in induction furnaces, and emissions are high.
Q: Compare casting, forging and machining as manufacturing processes.
Casting shapes a part by pouring liquid metal into a mould; it can produce very large, complex shapes with internal cavities, from any castable alloy, at low tooling cost for sand moulds, but the structure is coarse, may contain porosity and shrinkage defects, mechanical properties (particularly ductility and fatigue strength) are lower, and dimensional accuracy and finish are moderate except in precision processes. Forging shapes heated (or cold) metal by compressive plastic deformation; the grain flow follows the part contour and porosity is closed, giving superior strength, toughness and fatigue resistance – hence crankshafts, connecting rods, gears and hand tools are forged. Its limitations are the need for ductile materials, limited complexity (no internal cavities), high die costs and the need for machining of critical surfaces. Machining removes material with cutting tools to obtain precise dimensions, close tolerances and good surface finish on parts made by casting, forging or from bar stock; it is flexible and suits small quantities, but wastes material as chips, is slower, cuts across grain flow and becomes costly for hard materials. In practice the three are combined: a part is cast or forged near to shape and then machined on functional surfaces, the choice depending on shape, size, quantity, material and required properties.
Q: Explain the principle of ultrasonic machining with its applications.
Ultrasonic machining removes material from hard and brittle workpieces by the hammering action of abrasive grains. A transducer (magnetostrictive or piezoelectric) converts high-frequency electrical energy (about 20 kHz) into mechanical vibrations, which are amplified by a tapered horn (concentrator) to an amplitude of about 10–50 μm at the tool tip. The tool, made of a soft but tough material such as mild steel, brass or stainless steel and shaped as the required cavity, is held close to the workpiece under a light static feed force. A slurry of fine abrasive particles (boron carbide, silicon carbide or alumina in water) is circulated through the gap. The vibrating tool drives the abrasive grains against the work surface many thousands of times per second, chipping out minute particles by brittle fracture; cavitation and slurry flow assist removal. The tool wears slowly, so the work takes the negative shape of the tool. USM can machine both conductive and non-conductive materials, produces no heat-affected zone or chemical change and gives good accuracy, but the MRR is low, tool wear is significant and it is ineffective on soft ductile metals. Applications: drilling holes and cavities of any shape in glass, ceramics, quartz, precious stones, carbides and hardened steels; making dies, wire-drawing dies and coining tools; engraving; and machining semiconductor wafers.
Q: Explain work sampling and its advantages over stop-watch time study.
Work sampling (activity sampling or ratio-delay study), developed by L. H. C. Tippett, estimates the proportion of time that workers or machines spend on various activities by making a large number of instantaneous observations at random times, rather than continuous timing. It is based on the laws of probability: if an activity is observed in p fraction of the observations, it occupies approximately p fraction of the total time. The number of observations required for a given accuracy L at 95% confidence is N = 4p(1 − p)/L² (absolute accuracy). The procedure involves defining activities, making a preliminary study to estimate p, determining N, preparing a random schedule of observation times, recording observations, checking with control limits and computing the results; combined with output and rating data it can set standard times. Advantages over stop-watch study: suitable for long-cycle, non-repetitive and group work (maintenance, office work, material handling); one observer can cover many workers and machines; it needs no stop-watch or continuous presence, causes less fatigue and less operator resentment; observations can be interrupted without bias; and it is cheaper. Its disadvantages are that it is less suitable for short-cycle repetitive work, gives less detailed element breakdown, and requires a large number of observations for accuracy.
