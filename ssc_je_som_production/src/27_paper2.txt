## P1. Strength of Materials
Q: Derive the relation between E, G and μ.
Consider a square element ABCD of side a under pure shear stress τ. The diagonal AC elongates and BD shortens. Pure shear is equivalent to a tensile stress τ along one diagonal and a compressive stress τ along the other. The strain along AC is therefore e = τ/E + μτ/E = (τ/E)(1 + μ). Geometrically, the shear strain φ = τ/G distorts the square so that corner C moves horizontally by aφ; the extension of diagonal AC (length a√2) is aφ cos45° = aφ/√2, so the diagonal strain = (aφ/√2)/(a√2) = φ/2 = τ/(2G). Equating the two expressions for the diagonal strain: (τ/E)(1 + μ) = τ/(2G), which gives E = 2G(1 + μ). Similarly, by considering a cube under equal stresses σ on all faces, volumetric strain = 3(σ/E)(1 − 2μ) = σ/K, giving E = 3K(1 − 2μ). Eliminating μ between these yields E = 9KG/(3K + G). Since μ lies between 0 and 0.5, E lies between 2G and 3G.
Q: Explain the theory of pure torsion and state why hollow shafts are preferred to solid shafts.
In pure torsion a shaft carries a constant torque with no bending moment or axial load. Assuming that cross-sections remain plane and radii remain straight, the shear strain at radius r is rθ/L, so shear stress τ = Gθr/L increases linearly from zero at the axis to a maximum at the surface. Integrating the moment of the shear stresses over the section gives T = GθJ/L, leading to the torsion equation T/J = τ/r = Gθ/L. Because the material near the centre is lightly stressed and has a small lever arm, it contributes little to torque capacity. A hollow shaft removes this inefficiently used material, so for the same weight it has a larger polar moment of inertia and polar modulus: it can transmit more torque and is stiffer (smaller twist). For example, a hollow shaft with inner diameter half the outer has about 94% of the strength of a solid shaft of the same outer diameter but only 75% of its weight; for equal weight it is about 44% stronger. Hollow shafts also allow passage of oil or cables and have higher natural frequency. Their disadvantages are higher manufacturing cost and larger outside diameter for the same torque, so they are used where weight matters – aircraft, propeller shafts, machine tool spindles.
Q: What are the assumptions in Euler's column theory? State its limitations and how Rankine's formula overcomes them.
Euler's theory assumes that the column is initially perfectly straight and of uniform cross-section; the load is exactly axial; the material is homogeneous, isotropic and obeys Hooke's law; the column is long compared with its lateral dimensions so that it fails by buckling only; self-weight is neglected; and end conditions are ideal (pinned, fixed or free). It gives P = π²EI/L_e². Its limitations: it ignores direct compressive (crushing) stress, so for short and medium columns it predicts loads far higher than the crushing load; it is valid only when the critical stress π²E/λ² is below the proportional limit, i.e. for large slenderness ratios (above about 80–100 for mild steel); and real columns have initial crookedness, eccentric loading and imperfect end fixity, which reduce the load. Rankine's empirical formula combines crushing and buckling: 1/P_R = 1/P_c + 1/P_e, or P_R = σ_c A/(1 + aλ²). For short columns P_e is very large and P_R approaches the crushing load; for long columns P_e is small compared with P_c and P_R approaches the Euler load. Thus a single formula covers the whole range of slenderness, with the constant a determined from tests for each material.
Q: Explain the concept of the point of contraflexure with an example.
A point of contraflexure (or inflexion) is a point in a beam where the bending moment changes sign, from sagging to hogging, passing through zero. At this point the curvature of the elastic curve changes direction. It occurs only in beams where both sagging and hogging moments exist – overhanging beams, fixed beams, propped cantilevers and continuous beams – and not in simply supported beams with downward loads or in cantilevers. For example, a fixed beam carrying a uniformly distributed load w over span L has hogging moments wL²/12 at the supports and a sagging moment wL²/24 at the centre; setting the BM M(x) = wLx/2 − wx²/2 − wL²/12 = 0 gives x = 0.211L from each end, which are the points of contraflexure. Similarly, an overhanging beam with loads on the overhang has a point of contraflexure between the supports. These points are significant because the bending stress is zero there: they are suitable locations for joints or splices in continuous beams, and in reinforced concrete the main steel must be shifted from the bottom to the top across them.

## P2. Production Engineering
Q: Explain the iron–carbon equilibrium diagram with its important reactions.
The iron–iron carbide diagram shows the phases present in steels and cast irons at different carbon contents (0–6.67%) and temperatures under slow (equilibrium) cooling. Pure iron exists as δ-ferrite (BCC) above 1394 °C, austenite γ (FCC) between 1394 and 912 °C, and α-ferrite (BCC) below 912 °C. Austenite dissolves up to 2.14% carbon at 1147 °C; ferrite only 0.022% at 727 °C. Cementite (Fe₃C, 6.67% C) is a hard, brittle compound. Three invariant reactions occur: the peritectic at about 1495 °C and 0.17% C (δ + liquid → γ); the eutectic at 1147 °C and 4.3% C, where liquid transforms to ledeburite (γ + Fe₃C); and the eutectoid at 727 °C and 0.8% C, where austenite transforms to pearlite, a lamellar mixture of ferrite and cementite. Steels (below 2.14% C) are classified as hypo-eutectoid (ferrite + pearlite), eutectoid (pearlite) and hyper-eutectoid (pearlite + cementite); cast irons contain 2.14–6.67% C. The lines A₁ (727 °C), A₃ and A_cm define the critical temperatures used in heat treatment – for example, hypo-eutectoid steels are hardened from about 30–50 °C above A₃. The diagram does not show non-equilibrium products such as martensite and bainite, which are described by TTT diagrams.
Q: Describe the different types of chips formed in metal cutting.
(1) Continuous chips are long ribbons with a smooth shiny under-surface, formed when machining ductile materials (mild steel, copper, aluminium) at high cutting speed, with a large positive rake angle, small feed and depth, sharp tool and efficient cutting fluid. The material deforms plastically along the shear plane without fracture. They give good surface finish and low power consumption, but they are hazardous and difficult to dispose of, so chip breakers are used. (2) Continuous chips with built-up edge form when ductile materials are cut at low speeds with high friction at the tool–chip interface: work material welds to the rake face and builds up, periodically breaking off and sticking to the chip and machined surface, giving poor finish and altering the tool geometry; higher speed, larger rake, better lubrication and polished tools reduce it. (3) Discontinuous (segmental) chips are small separate segments formed when machining brittle materials such as cast iron, brass and bronze, or ductile materials at very low speed with small rake and large feed; they are easy to handle and the finish is fair in brittle materials, but force fluctuations can cause vibration. (4) Serrated or saw-tooth chips form with low-conductivity, hard materials (titanium alloys, hardened steels) at high speeds due to adiabatic shear bands.
Q: Explain the gating system of a sand mould and the functions of each element.
The gating system leads molten metal from the ladle into the mould cavity in a controlled way. The pouring basin (cup) receives the metal, reduces its momentum and helps keep slag back; a skim core or strainer may be included. The sprue is a vertical passage leading metal down to the parting level; it is tapered (larger at the top) so that, as the stream accelerates, it stays in contact with the walls, avoiding aspiration of air and gas into the metal. The sprue well at its base reduces the velocity and turbulence where the flow turns horizontal. The runner carries metal horizontally, usually in the drag or cope, and is extended beyond the last gate to trap the first cold, dirty metal; skim bobs trap slag. The ingates connect the runner to the cavity – top, parting-line, bottom or step gates – and control the rate, direction and distribution of metal entry. The gating ratio (sprue : runner : gate area) determines whether the system is pressurised (choke at gates, used for ferrous metals) or unpressurised (choke at sprue, used for aluminium and magnesium to reduce turbulence and oxidation). Risers, though not part of the gating proper, are connected to supply feed metal as the casting shrinks. A good gating system fills the mould quickly without turbulence, erosion or cold shuts, removes slag and promotes directional solidification towards the risers.
Q: Explain the various methods of taper turning on a lathe.
(1) Form tool method: a broad-nosed tool with its cutting edge set at the half-taper angle is fed straight in; suitable only for short tapers (chamfers) because long edges cause chatter. (2) Compound rest (swivelling) method: the compound rest is swivelled to the half-taper angle α (tan α = (D − d)/2L) and the tool is fed by hand along it; suitable for short, steep tapers, both external and internal, because the travel of the compound slide is limited. (3) Tailstock set-over method: the tailstock body is offset laterally so that the axis of the job held between centres is inclined to the lathe axis; the tool is fed by the carriage parallel to the bed. The set-over S = (D − d)L/(2l), where L is the total job length and l the taper length. It is used for long, gentle external tapers but not for internal or steep tapers, and the centres do not bear properly. (4) Taper-turning attachment: a guide bar at the back of the lathe set at the half-taper angle guides the cross-slide (disconnected from its screw) as the carriage moves, so the tool follows the taper; it gives accurate long tapers, internal and external, with the work on centres or in a chuck. (5) Combined feed method: the tool is moved simultaneously by longitudinal and cross feeds. CNC lathes produce tapers directly by linear interpolation.
Q: Distinguish between jigs and fixtures and state the principles of their design.
A jig is a device that holds and locates the workpiece and also guides the cutting tool to the correct position, usually through hardened drill bushes; jigs are used for drilling, reaming and tapping, and they are generally lighter and are not clamped to the machine table. A fixture holds and locates the work but does not guide the tool; the tool position is set by setting blocks and feeler gauges, and the fixture is firmly bolted to the machine table. Fixtures are used in milling, turning, grinding, welding, assembly and inspection. Design principles common to both: locate the workpiece positively using the 3-2-1 principle on machined datum surfaces, avoiding redundant location; provide rigid support against cutting forces, with clamps acting directly over supports and not on unsupported areas, so the work does not distort; ensure quick loading and unloading with simple, fast-acting clamps (cam, toggle, quick-release); make the design fool-proof so the part can be loaded only in the correct orientation; provide chip clearance and coolant drainage, and relief at corners for burrs; use hardened, replaceable wear parts (bushes, locators); keep the device light but rigid, and safe to operate. Using jigs and fixtures reduces marking out, ensures interchangeability, permits less skilled labour and increases productivity in batch and mass production.
