## P3. Strength of Materials
Q: Derive the expression for strain energy stored in a body due to shear and state the strain energy in a solid shaft under torsion.
Consider a rectangular block of height h, area A of the top face, fixed at the bottom and subjected to a shear force P on the top face applied gradually. The top face moves through a distance δ = φh, where the shear strain φ = τ/G. Work done = ½Pδ = ½(τA)(φh) = ½τφ(Ah) = (τ²/2G) × V, where V = Ah is the volume. This work is stored as shear strain energy, so the energy per unit volume is τ²/(2G). In a solid circular shaft under torque, the shear stress varies linearly from zero at the centre to τ_max at the surface: τ_r = τ_max r/R. The energy in an elemental ring of radius r, thickness dr and length L is (τ_r²/2G) × 2πr dr L. Integrating from 0 to R: U = (τ_max²/2GR²) × 2πL ∫r³ dr = (τ_max²/2GR²) × 2πL × R⁴/4 = (τ_max²/4G) × πR²L = (τ_max²/4G) × V. Thus a solid shaft stores only half the energy it would if the whole volume were stressed to τ_max. For a hollow shaft U = (τ²/4G) × [(D² + d²)/D²] × V, which is higher per unit volume – another reason hollow shafts are efficient for absorbing energy.
Q: Explain the different methods of finding the slope and deflection of beams.
(1) Double integration: the differential equation EI d²y/dx² = M is integrated twice; constants are found from boundary conditions (y = 0 at supports, dy/dx = 0 at fixed ends). It is direct for simple loading but needs separate equations for each segment when loads are discontinuous. (2) Macaulay's method: a single BM expression valid for the whole beam is written using Macaulay brackets ⟨x − a⟩ that are taken as zero when negative; integration is done without opening the brackets, so only two constants are needed. It is convenient for several point loads. (3) Moment-area method: Mohr's theorems state that the change of slope between two points equals the area of the M/EI diagram between them, and the tangential deviation equals the moment of that area; it is quick for cantilevers and symmetrically loaded beams. (4) Conjugate beam method: the real beam's M/EI diagram is applied as load on an imaginary conjugate beam with modified supports (fixed ↔ free, simple support unchanged); the shear and BM in the conjugate beam give the slope and deflection of the real beam. (5) Energy methods – Castigliano's theorem and the unit-load (virtual work) method – are useful for curved members, frames and beams of varying section. (6) Superposition of standard results from tables for combined loads.
Q: Explain the concept of a beam of uniform strength with an example.
A beam of uniform strength is one in which the maximum bending stress at every cross-section is the same and equal to the allowable stress. Since σ = M/Z, the section modulus must vary along the length in proportion to the bending moment. This makes the most economical use of material and reduces weight. For example, a cantilever carrying a point load W at the free end has M = Wx at distance x from the free end. If the depth d is kept constant, Z = bd²/6 ∝ b, so the width must vary linearly with x: b = 6Wx/(σd²) – the plan is triangular. If the width is constant, d must vary as √x – a parabolic profile. For a simply supported beam with a central load and constant width, the depth varies as √x from each support. A laminated leaf spring is a practical beam of uniform strength: a triangular plate of uniform thickness (constant stress) is cut into strips and stacked, giving leaves of decreasing length. Other examples are tapered cantilever brackets, girders with cover plates added near mid-span, and fish-bellied beams. In practice a minimum section is kept near supports to carry shear.

## P4. Production Engineering
Q: Explain the principle and applications of electrochemical machining (ECM).
ECM is based on Faraday's laws of electrolysis – reverse electroplating. The workpiece is made the anode and a shaped tool the cathode of a DC circuit (low voltage 5–30 V, very high current density 10–100 A/cm²); a conductive electrolyte, usually sodium chloride or sodium nitrate solution, is pumped at high velocity through the small gap (0.1–0.6 mm) between them. Metal atoms at the anode surface dissolve as ions, forming hydroxides that are flushed away as sludge, while hydrogen gas is evolved at the cathode; the tool itself is not eroded. As the tool is fed towards the work at constant rate, an equilibrium gap is established and the work takes the mirror shape of the tool. The material removal rate depends on current and on the electrochemical equivalent of the metal, not on its hardness: MRR = AI/(ρnF). Advantages: any conductive material regardless of hardness can be machined; there is no tool wear; complex three-dimensional shapes are produced in one pass; no residual stresses, burrs or heat-affected zone; good surface finish. Limitations: high equipment cost, large power requirement, corrosion and disposal problems with electrolyte, inability to produce sharp internal corners, and only conductive materials. Applications: turbine blades and blisks, die cavities, rifling of gun barrels, deburring (electrochemical deburring), drilling small and deep holes, and electrochemical grinding of carbide tools.
Q: Explain Taylor's tool life equation and the factors affecting tool life.
Tool life is the time a tool cuts satisfactorily before it must be reground or replaced, usually defined by a limiting flank wear land (e.g. VB = 0.3 mm) or by crater depth, failure or deterioration of finish. F. W. Taylor found experimentally that VTⁿ = C, where V is the cutting speed, T the tool life in minutes, n an exponent depending mainly on the tool material (about 0.1–0.15 for HSS, 0.2–0.4 for carbides and 0.5–0.7 for ceramics) and C a constant equal to the cutting speed for one-minute life. On a log–log plot the relation is a straight line of slope −n. The extended form VTⁿf^a d^b = K shows that feed and depth of cut also reduce tool life, but less than speed, and depth least of all. Factors affecting tool life: cutting speed (most important, since it controls temperature); feed and depth of cut; tool material and its hot hardness and wear resistance; tool geometry – rake, clearance, nose radius, approach angle; work material – hardness, abrasive inclusions, work hardening; cutting fluid; rigidity of machine and set-up (vibration and chatter); and type of cut (continuous or interrupted). Tool life is used to select economical cutting speeds: the speed for minimum cost or maximum production rate is obtained from Taylor's equation together with tool and machine costs.
Q: Explain the various methods of control charts used in statistical quality control.
Control charts, introduced by Shewhart, plot a quality characteristic of samples taken at intervals against control limits normally placed at ±3σ from the centre line, so that, when only chance causes act, 99.73% of points fall within them. A point outside the limits, or a non-random pattern (runs, trends), indicates an assignable cause to be investigated. Charts for variables (measurable characteristics such as diameter or weight): the X̄ chart monitors the process mean, with limits X̿ ± A₂R̄; the R chart monitors variability using D₃R̄ and D₄R̄; the s chart uses standard deviation for larger samples. They are used together. Charts for attributes (counted characteristics): the p chart plots fraction defective, limits p̄ ± 3√(p̄(1 − p̄)/n), and can handle variable sample sizes; the np chart plots number defective for constant n; the c chart plots number of defects per unit (Poisson distribution), limits c̄ ± 3√c̄, used for defects on castings, cloth, or painted surfaces; the u chart plots defects per unit for varying unit sizes. Variable charts give more information per sample and detect shifts earlier; attribute charts are simpler and cover many characteristics at once. Control charts help to achieve and maintain statistical control, estimate process capability and reduce scrap and inspection costs.
Q: Explain the types of plant layout and their suitability.
Plant layout is the physical arrangement of machines, work centres, storage and services to allow smooth flow of materials at minimum cost. (1) Product or line layout arranges machines in the sequence of operations required by a product, as in automobile assembly lines and bottling plants. It suits mass production of standardised products with stable demand; material handling and work-in-process are minimal, control is simple and throughput high, but it is inflexible, requires high investment, and a breakdown of one machine stops the line; line balancing is essential. (2) Process or functional layout groups similar machines together – lathes in one section, milling machines in another – as in job shops, hospitals and tool rooms. It handles a variety of products in small batches, offers flexibility and better machine utilisation, and breakdowns affect only a part, but material handling, WIP and throughput time are high and production control is complex. (3) Fixed-position layout keeps the product stationary while workers, materials and equipment move to it – ship building, aircraft and locomotive assembly, large turbines, construction – used when the product is very large or heavy. (4) Cellular or group-technology layout forms cells of dissimilar machines to process families of similar parts, combining the efficiency of product layout with the flexibility of process layout for medium variety and volume. (5) Combination layouts mix these types.
