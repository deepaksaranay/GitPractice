# Paper-II Model Answers – Set 2
More conventional-style answers covering topics that frequently appear in descriptive papers.

## P7. Thermodynamics and Cycles
Q: Prove the equivalence of the Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements.
The two statements are equivalent if violation of one implies violation of the other. (a) Suppose the Clausius statement is violated: a device transfers heat Q₂ from a cold reservoir to a hot reservoir with no work input. Let an ordinary heat engine operate between the same reservoirs, taking Q₁ from the hot reservoir, producing W = Q₁ − Q₂ and rejecting Q₂ to the cold reservoir. Combining the two, the cold reservoir has no net heat exchange; the hot reservoir supplies net heat Q₁ − Q₂ and the combination produces equal work. This is a cyclic device that converts heat from a single reservoir completely into work – a violation of the Kelvin–Planck statement. (b) Suppose the Kelvin–Planck statement is violated: an engine converts Q₁ from the hot reservoir fully into work W = Q₁. Let this work drive a refrigerator that takes Q₂ from the cold reservoir and delivers Q₁ + Q₂ to the hot reservoir. The net effect is transfer of Q₂ from the cold to the hot reservoir with no external work – a violation of the Clausius statement. Since each violation implies the other, the statements are equivalent.
Q: Compare Otto, Diesel and Dual cycles for the same compression ratio and heat input, and for the same maximum pressure and temperature.
For the same compression ratio and the same heat input, the three cycles share the same compression process. On the T–s diagram they reject heat along the same constant-volume line; the Otto cycle adds all heat at constant volume, reaching the highest temperature, and therefore rejects the least heat; the Diesel cycle adds heat at constant pressure and rejects the most heat; the dual cycle lies between them. Since η = 1 − Q₂/Q₁, the order is η_Otto > η_Dual > η_Diesel. For the same maximum pressure and maximum temperature (a better comparison of real engines, because the limits are set by strength and materials), the Diesel cycle must have a much higher compression ratio; the heat rejected is the same for all, but the Diesel cycle receives the most heat, so η_Diesel > η_Dual > η_Otto. This explains why actual CI engines are more efficient than SI engines: they work at higher compression ratios, which SI engines cannot use because of detonation.

## P8. Boilers, Nozzles and Turbines
Q: Explain boiler mountings and accessories with the function of each.
Mountings are fittings mounted on the boiler for its safe operation and control; accessories are auxiliary devices that improve efficiency. Mountings: (1) Water-level indicator (gauge glass) – shows the water level in the drum; (2) Pressure gauge (Bourdon tube) – indicates steam pressure; (3) Safety valves (dead-weight, lever, spring-loaded, high-steam low-water) – release steam when pressure exceeds the safe limit; (4) Fusible plug – a plug with low-melting alloy above the furnace crown that melts when the water level falls dangerously low, letting steam and water extinguish the fire; (5) Steam stop valve – regulates steam flow from the boiler to the steam pipe; (6) Feed check valve – non-return valve that admits feed water and prevents back-flow; (7) Blow-off cock – removes sludge and sediment from the bottom and empties the boiler; (8) Manholes and mud holes for inspection and cleaning. Accessories: (1) Economiser – heats feed water using flue gas; (2) Air preheater – heats combustion air using flue gas; (3) Superheater – converts wet/saturated steam to superheated; (4) Feed pump and injector – supply water to the boiler against its pressure; (5) Steam separator and steam trap – remove water from steam and drain condensate; (6) Draught fans and chimney; (7) Soot blowers. The order of flue gas passage is superheater → economiser → air preheater.
Q: Derive the expression for critical pressure ratio in a nozzle.
For isentropic flow of a gas or superheated steam obeying pvⁿ = C, the steady-flow energy equation with negligible inlet velocity gives V₂² /2 = h₁ − h₂ = [n/(n − 1)] p₁v₁[1 − (p₂/p₁)^((n−1)/n)]. The mass flow per unit area is ṁ/A = V₂/v₂, with v₂ = v₁(p₁/p₂)^(1/n). Substituting, ṁ/A = √{[2n/(n − 1)](p₁/v₁)[(p₂/p₁)^(2/n) − (p₂/p₁)^((n+1)/n)]}. For a given inlet state, ṁ/A is maximum (throat condition) when the bracketed term is maximum. Differentiating with respect to r = p₂/p₁ and equating to zero: (2/n) r^((2−n)/n) = ((n + 1)/n) r^(1/n), which gives r_c = (2/(n + 1))^(n/(n−1)). For air (n = γ = 1.4) r_c = 0.528; for superheated steam (n = 1.3) r_c = 0.546; for dry saturated steam (n = 1.135) r_c = 0.577. At this pressure ratio the velocity at the throat equals the local sonic velocity; further lowering of back pressure does not increase the mass flow – the nozzle is choked.
Q: Differentiate between impulse and reaction turbines.
In an impulse turbine the entire pressure drop of a stage occurs in the fixed nozzles; steam enters the moving blades at high velocity and constant pressure, and the blades, being symmetrical, only change the direction of the jet – the force comes from the change of momentum. In a reaction turbine the pressure drops in both fixed and moving blades (the blade passages are converging nozzles), so the steam accelerates relative to the moving blades and exerts a reaction force in addition to the impulse. Consequences: impulse blades are symmetrical with constant-area passages, reaction blades are aerofoil-shaped with converging passages; in impulse turbines pressure is constant across the rotor, so tip leakage is small and partial admission is possible; reaction turbines need full admission, have pressure difference across the moving blades, require balance pistons/dummy pistons for axial thrust and close tip clearances; impulse stages handle a larger enthalpy drop per stage so fewer stages are needed, while reaction turbines have more stages and are more efficient at their design point. Optimum blade speed ratio is cosα/2 for impulse and cosα for 50% reaction stages. Examples: De Laval, Curtis and Rateau (impulse); Parsons (reaction).

## P9. IC Engines, Power Plants, Compressors
Q: Explain the necessity of cooling in IC engines and compare air and water cooling.
During combustion gas temperatures reach about 2000–2500 °C; without cooling, the cylinder walls, piston and valves would overheat, losing strength and causing thermal stresses, distortion and seizure of the piston; the lubricating oil film would break down and burn; overheated surfaces and spark plugs would cause pre-ignition and detonation in SI engines; and volumetric efficiency would fall because the incoming charge is heated. About 25–35% of fuel energy is removed by the cooling system. Over-cooling must also be avoided, as it increases heat loss, fuel consumption, friction (viscous oil) and corrosion from condensed acids. Air cooling uses fins on the cylinder and head, with natural airflow or a fan (motorcycles, small engines, aircraft piston engines): it is light, simple, cheap, needs no radiator or coolant and warms up quickly, but cooling is less uniform, noisier, and limited to small engines. Water (liquid) cooling circulates coolant through jackets around the cylinders by a pump (forced circulation) or thermosiphon, with a thermostat to control temperature and a radiator with fan to reject heat; it gives uniform and effective cooling for large and high-output engines, quieter operation and allows higher compression ratios, but adds weight, cost, maintenance and the risk of leaks and freezing (antifreeze such as ethylene glycol is added).
Q: Explain the working of a nuclear power plant with its main components.
A nuclear power plant generates heat by controlled fission of U-235 (or Pu-239) and uses it to raise steam for a conventional turbine-generator. Main components of the reactor: (1) Fuel – natural or enriched uranium as UO₂ pellets in zircaloy tubes; (2) Moderator – slows fast neutrons to thermal energies (heavy water, light water, graphite); (3) Control rods – absorb neutrons to regulate or shut down the chain reaction (boron, cadmium, hafnium); (4) Coolant – removes heat from the core (pressurised water, heavy water, CO₂, liquid sodium); (5) Reflector – returns escaping neutrons to the core; (6) Reactor vessel and biological shield (thick concrete) to protect personnel from radiation. In a pressurised water reactor (PWR) the primary coolant is kept at about 155 bar so it does not boil; it transfers heat to a secondary circuit in a steam generator, and the steam drives the turbine. In a boiling water reactor (BWR) steam is generated in the reactor itself. Indian PHWRs use natural uranium with heavy water as moderator and coolant. Advantages include very low fuel requirement and no CO₂ emission; disadvantages are high capital cost, radioactive waste disposal, safety concerns and the need for skilled operation.
Q: What is supercharging? Explain its effects on SI and CI engines.
Supercharging is supplying air (or mixture) to an engine at a pressure above atmospheric, so that a greater mass of charge is inducted per cycle and more fuel can be burned, increasing power output for the same engine size. The supercharger may be mechanically driven by the crankshaft (Roots blower, vane or centrifugal compressor) or driven by an exhaust-gas turbine (turbocharger), which uses otherwise wasted exhaust energy. Objectives: to increase power-to-weight ratio, to compensate for loss of power at high altitude (aircraft), and to reduce engine size for a given power. In CI engines supercharging is very beneficial: the higher pressure and temperature of the charge reduce ignition delay, giving smoother combustion and reduced diesel knock, and excess air improves combustion; it is limited mainly by thermal and mechanical loads. In SI engines, supercharging increases the tendency to detonate because the end gas is at a higher pressure and temperature; it is therefore used with lower compression ratios, intercooling and high-octane fuels. Intercooling (charge-air cooling) after the supercharger increases charge density further and reduces thermal load and NOx.

## P10. Refrigeration, AC and Heat Transfer
Q: Explain the desirable properties of a refrigerant.
A good refrigerant should have (1) a low boiling point at atmospheric pressure, so that low temperatures are obtained at pressures above atmospheric, preventing air leakage into the system; (2) a moderate condensing pressure to keep equipment light; (3) high latent heat of vaporisation to give a large refrigerating effect per kg and low mass flow; (4) low specific volume of vapour so that the compressor is small; (5) a high critical temperature, well above the condensing temperature, and a low freezing point; (6) high COP and low power per TR; (7) high thermal conductivity and low viscosity for good heat transfer and low pressure drop; (8) chemical stability, non-toxicity, non-flammability and non-corrosiveness; (9) miscibility or compatibility with lubricating oil, and high dielectric strength for hermetic compressors; (10) ease of leak detection; (11) zero ozone depletion potential and low global-warming potential; (12) low cost and easy availability. No refrigerant has all these properties – ammonia has excellent thermodynamic properties but is toxic and attacks copper; halocarbons are safe but have environmental drawbacks – so the choice depends on the application.
Q: Explain the electrical analogy of heat conduction through a composite wall.
Steady heat conduction obeys Q = ΔT/R, analogous to Ohm's law I = ΔV/R, where heat flow corresponds to current, temperature difference to potential difference, and thermal resistance to electrical resistance. A plane layer has conduction resistance L/(kA), a convective surface film 1/(hA), and a cylindrical layer ln(r₂/r₁)/(2πkL). For a composite wall of layers in series – for example inside film, plaster, brick, insulation, outside film – the same heat flows through each layer, so the resistances add: R_total = 1/(h₁A) + ΣLᵢ/(kᵢA) + 1/(h₂A), and Q = (T_i − T_o)/R_total. The temperature drop across each layer is proportional to its resistance, which allows interface temperatures to be calculated. When different materials are placed side by side (parallel paths, such as a wall with a window or studs), the conductances add: 1/R_eq = Σ1/Rᵢ. The overall heat transfer coefficient U is defined by 1/(UA) = R_total. The analogy simplifies the analysis of insulated pipes, building walls and heat exchanger tubes, and extends to radiation networks using surface and space resistances.
