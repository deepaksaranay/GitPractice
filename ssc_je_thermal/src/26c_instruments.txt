# Thermal Measurements and Instruments

## 26C.1 Temperature Measurement
| Instrument | Principle | Range / remarks |
| Liquid-in-glass (mercury) | Thermal expansion of liquid | −39 to ≈ 357 °C (mercury); alcohol to −110 °C |
| Bimetallic strip | Unequal expansion of two bonded metals (e.g. invar + brass) | Thermostats, dial thermometers; −30 to 500 °C |
| Pressure (filled-system) thermometer | Gas/vapour/liquid pressure rises with temperature | Remote dial reading; vapour type has non-linear scale |
| Thermocouple | Seebeck effect – emf at junction of dissimilar metals depends on temperature difference | −200 to 1800 °C; fast, cheap, small; needs cold-junction compensation |
| Resistance temperature detector (RTD) | Resistance of metal increases with temperature (positive coefficient) | Platinum Pt-100 (100 Ω at 0 °C, α ≈ 0.00385 /°C); −200 to 850 °C; most accurate and stable |
| Thermistor | Semiconductor resistance – usually decreases sharply with temperature (NTC) | Very sensitive, non-linear; −50 to 300 °C |
| Total radiation pyrometer | Stefan–Boltzmann law – total radiation focused on thermopile | High temperatures, non-contact |
| Optical (disappearing-filament) pyrometer | Brightness of filament matched with target at one wavelength (red, 0.65 μm) | 700–3000 °C, furnace and molten metal temperatures |
| Infrared thermometer / thermal camera | Infrared radiation intensity | Non-contact, needs emissivity setting |

| Thermocouple type | Materials | Feature |
| K | Chromel–Alumel | Most common general purpose, −200 to 1250 °C |
| J | Iron–Constantan | 0 to 750 °C; reducing atmospheres |
| T | Copper–Constantan | Low temperatures (−200 to 350 °C), cryogenic |
| E | Chromel–Constantan | Highest emf per degree (sensitivity) |
| R, S | Platinum–Rhodium/Platinum | Up to ≈ 1600 °C, standards, high accuracy |
| B | Pt-30%Rh / Pt-6%Rh | Up to ≈ 1800 °C |
- Laws of thermocouples: (1) law of homogeneous circuit – emf depends only on junction temperatures, not on temperature distribution along wires; (2) law of intermediate metals – a third metal inserted at uniform temperature does not change the emf (allows meters and leads); (3) law of intermediate temperatures – E(T₁,T₃) = E(T₁,T₂) + E(T₂,T₃) (allows cold-junction correction).
- Thermopile: several thermocouples in series to increase output (radiation pyrometers). Peltier effect (heat absorbed/released at a junction when current flows) and Thomson effect (heat in a single conductor with temperature gradient and current) are related thermoelectric effects.
PYQ-TREND: Thermocouple works on Seebeck effect (very frequent); RTD platinum; thermistor NTC; optical pyrometer for very high temperature (non-contact); laws of thermocouples; type T for low temperature.

## 26C.2 Pressure Measurement
| Instrument | Principle / use |
| Manometers (U-tube, inclined, well type) | Liquid column; low pressures and differentials; calibration |
| Bourdon tube gauge | Elastic C, spiral or helical tube straightens under pressure; most common industrial gauge (boilers) |
| Diaphragm gauge | Elastic deflection of diaphragm; corrosive fluids, low pressures |
| Bellows gauge | Expansion of bellows; low pressures/differentials |
| Dead-weight tester | Known weights on a piston of known area – calibration of gauges |
| Piezoelectric transducer | Charge generated by crystal under stress – rapidly varying (dynamic) pressure such as engine cylinder pressure |
| Strain-gauge transducer | Diaphragm strain changes resistance |
| McLeod gauge | Compression of a known gas volume – vacuum down to ≈ 10⁻⁴ mm Hg; used to calibrate other vacuum gauges |
| Pirani gauge / thermocouple gauge | Thermal conductivity of gas varies with pressure (vacuum) |
| Ionisation gauge | Ion current – very high vacuum (to 10⁻¹⁰ mm Hg) |
PYQ-TREND: Bourdon tube for boiler pressure (mounting); dead-weight tester for calibration; McLeod gauge for vacuum; piezoelectric for dynamic pressure; Pirani gauge works on thermal conductivity.

## 26C.3 Flow, Speed and Power Measurement
| Quantity | Instruments |
| Flow rate | Venturimeter, orifice meter, flow nozzle (differential pressure); rotameter (variable area, vertical); turbine meter; electromagnetic meter (conducting liquids, Faraday's law); ultrasonic meter; positive-displacement meters (fuel, water) |
| Air flow into engine | Air box with orifice (damps pulsations), hot-wire/hot-film mass airflow sensor, viscous flow meter |
| Fuel consumption | Burette and stop-watch (volumetric), gravimetric balance, flow meters |
| Velocity | Pitot tube, hot-wire anemometer (turbulence), cup and vane anemometers (wind, ducts), laser Doppler anemometer |
| Speed | Mechanical/electrical tachometers, magnetic pick-ups, optical encoders, stroboscope (non-contact) |
### Dynamometers (brake power)
| Type | Examples | Remarks |
| Absorption | Prony brake, rope brake | Friction; simple, low cost, small engines; heat dissipated by water cooling |
| Absorption | Hydraulic (Froude) dynamometer | Water churned between rotor and casing; high power, large engines |
| Absorption | Eddy-current dynamometer | Eddy currents in a rotating disc in magnetic field; easy control, wide speed range |
| Transmission | Torsion dynamometer, belt dynamometer | Measure torque transmitted without absorbing power |
| Driving | Swinging-field DC dynamometer | Can absorb power or motor the engine (motoring test for friction power) |
- Engine indicator: mechanical indicator (piston, spring and stylus) for slow-speed engines gives the indicator diagram; for high-speed engines, piezoelectric pressure transducer with crank-angle encoder and data acquisition. Area of indicator diagram measured by planimeter → imep → IP.
- Exhaust measurement: NDIR (CO, CO₂), FID (unburnt hydrocarbons), chemiluminescence (NOx), Orsat apparatus (CO₂, O₂, CO); smoke meters – Bosch (filter paper blackening) and Hartridge (light absorption) for diesel smoke.
PYQ-TREND: Rope brake and hydraulic dynamometers measure BP; eddy-current dynamometer; swinging-field dynamometer for motoring (friction power); indicator diagram gives IP; rotameter variable area; Bosch/Hartridge smoke meters; FID for HC, chemiluminescence for NOx.

## 26C.4 Humidity, Heat and Property Measurement
| Quantity | Instruments |
| Humidity | Sling psychrometer (DBT and WBT by whirling), Assmann aspiration psychrometer (fan-driven), hair hygrometer (hair length changes with humidity), dew-point hygrometer (cooled mirror), electronic capacitive sensors |
| Calorific value | Bomb calorimeter (solids, liquids), Junkers/Boys gas calorimeter |
| Dryness fraction | Barrel, separating, throttling, combined separating–throttling, electrical calorimeters |
| Thermal conductivity | Guarded hot-plate (insulators, flat specimens), Searle's apparatus (good conductors – metals bar), Lee's disc (poor conductors), transient hot-wire method (fluids) |
| Heat flux | Heat flux sensors (thermopile across thin layer), Gardon gauge (radiation) |
| Emissivity | Comparison with black body at same temperature |
| Viscosity of oils | Redwood, Saybolt, Engler viscometers |
| Flash and fire point | Pensky–Martens closed cup, Abel apparatus, Cleveland open cup |
- Boiler instruments: water-level gauge glass, pressure gauge (Bourdon), safety valves, fusible plug, feed check valve; steam flow meters; flue gas analysers (O₂, CO₂) for combustion control; draught gauges (inclined manometers measuring mm of water).
PYQ-TREND: Sling psychrometer measures DBT and WBT; guarded hot plate for thermal conductivity of insulators; Pensky–Martens apparatus for flash point; Redwood viscometer; hair hygrometer.
TIP: Matching rule for instruments: 'Seebeck – thermocouple; Peltier – thermoelectric cooler; Stefan–Boltzmann – total radiation pyrometer; brightness at one wavelength – optical pyrometer'.
Q: Explain the working of a thermocouple and state its advantages and limitations.
A thermocouple consists of two wires of dissimilar metals joined at two junctions. When the junctions are at different temperatures, an electromotive force proportional (approximately) to the temperature difference is produced – the Seebeck effect. The measuring (hot) junction is placed at the point whose temperature is required, while the reference (cold) junction is kept at a known temperature (ice bath at 0 °C) or compensated electronically. The emf, of the order of microvolts per degree (about 41 μV/°C for type K), is measured by a millivoltmeter or potentiometer and converted to temperature using standard tables. Advantages: wide range (−200 to 1800 °C depending on type), small size and low thermal mass (fast response), robust and cheap, output is electrical and suitable for remote reading, recording and control; several can be connected in series as a thermopile. Limitations: low output requiring amplification, non-linear characteristics, need for cold-junction compensation, lower accuracy than RTDs, susceptibility to electrical noise and to contamination or ageing at high temperatures.
