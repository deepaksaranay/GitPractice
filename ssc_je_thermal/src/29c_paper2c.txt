# Paper-II Model Answers – Set 3

## P11. Thermodynamics and Steam
Q: Explain the measurement of dryness fraction of steam by a combined separating and throttling calorimeter.
A throttling calorimeter alone works only if steam becomes superheated after throttling, which requires the initial dryness to be high (about 0.95 or more). For wetter steam a separating calorimeter is placed before it. Steam from the main first enters the separating calorimeter, where a sudden change in direction makes the heavier water particles separate by inertia and collect in a chamber (mass m₁ measured from a gauge glass). The partly dried steam (mass m₂, measured by condensing it after the throttling calorimeter) then passes through a narrow orifice into the throttling calorimeter, where it is throttled to nearly atmospheric pressure and becomes superheated; its pressure and temperature are measured. Since throttling is isenthalpic, h_f1 + x₂ h_fg1 = h_sup2 at the calorimeter pressure and temperature, giving x₂. The dryness fraction indicated by the separator is x₁ = m₂/(m₁ + m₂). The overall dryness fraction of the steam is x = x₁ × x₂. The combined arrangement measures dryness of very wet steam accurately, the separator removing most of the water and the throttling calorimeter measuring the remaining wetness.
Q: Explain the Mollier (h–s) diagram and its uses.
The Mollier chart plots specific enthalpy on the vertical axis against specific entropy on the horizontal axis for steam. It shows the saturation line (separating wet and superheated regions), constant-pressure lines (straight in the wet region, curving upward in the superheated region), constant-temperature lines (coinciding with constant-pressure lines in the wet region and becoming nearly horizontal at low pressures in the superheated region), constant-dryness lines below the saturation curve and constant-volume lines. Its main advantage is that isentropic processes are vertical lines and enthalpy changes are read directly as vertical distances. It is therefore used for quick solution of steam-turbine and nozzle problems: finding the isentropic enthalpy drop and exit dryness, actual exit state using isentropic efficiency, reheat and regenerative cycle states, throttling (horizontal lines), and calculation of nozzle velocities. It avoids interpolation in steam tables and gives a clear visual picture of the processes.

## P12. IC Engines
Q: Explain the working of a simple carburettor and its limitations.
A simple carburettor consists of a float chamber, a fuel jet (nozzle) with metering orifice, a venturi, and a throttle valve. The float and needle valve maintain a constant fuel level slightly below the jet tip. During the suction stroke air flows through the venturi; its velocity increases and pressure decreases at the throat. The pressure difference between the float chamber (atmospheric) and the throat forces fuel out of the jet, where it is atomised by the high-velocity air and partly vaporised, forming the air–fuel mixture. The throttle valve controls the quantity of mixture entering the engine, and hence the power (quantity governing). A choke valve upstream of the venturi enriches the mixture for cold starting. Limitations of the simple carburettor: as air flow increases, the mixture becomes progressively richer because fuel flow rises faster than air flow (air density falls with velocity, and fuel is incompressible); it cannot give the rich mixture needed at idle and for acceleration and full power, nor the lean mixture needed for economy; it is affected by altitude. Practical carburettors therefore add compensating jets, idle systems, accelerating pumps, power (economiser) systems and choke; modern engines have replaced carburettors with electronic fuel injection.
Q: Explain the different lubrication systems used in IC engines.
Lubrication reduces friction and wear, cools moving parts, seals the piston rings, removes contaminants and prevents corrosion. (1) Petroil (mist) lubrication: used in small two-stroke petrol engines – lubricating oil mixed with petrol (2–3%) enters with the charge through the crankcase and lubricates bearings and cylinder walls; simple but causes carbon deposits, smoke and oil waste. Modern two-strokes use separate oil injection. (2) Splash lubrication: a dipper on the connecting rod dips into the oil sump and splashes oil onto cylinder walls and bearings; used in small single-cylinder engines; uneven supply. (3) Pressure (forced-feed) lubrication: a gear or rotor pump draws oil from the sump through a strainer and delivers it under pressure (2–4 bar) through a filter to main bearings, crankpin bearings (via drilled crankshaft), camshaft and gudgeon pins; cylinder walls are lubricated by oil thrown from bearings; used in all modern multi-cylinder engines. A pressure-relief valve, oil cooler and pressure gauge are included. (4) Dry-sump system: oil is scavenged from a shallow sump by a separate pump to an external tank; used in racing cars, aircraft and off-road vehicles to prevent starvation during manoeuvres. Lubricating oil properties: suitable viscosity and viscosity index, oiliness, high flash and fire point, low pour point, oxidation stability and detergency.

## P13. Power Plant and Compressors
Q: Describe the layout of a modern coal-fired thermal power plant.
A coal-fired plant consists of several circuits. (1) Coal and ash circuit: coal received by rail is unloaded, crushed, stored, and fed through bunkers to pulverising mills that grind it to fine powder; it is blown with hot primary air into the furnace burners. Bottom ash collects below the furnace and fly ash is captured by electrostatic precipitators; ash is disposed of by hydraulic or pneumatic systems and increasingly used in cement and bricks. (2) Air and flue gas circuit: forced-draught fans push air through the air preheater into the furnace; flue gases pass over the superheater, reheater, economiser and air preheater, then through the ESP and flue-gas desulphurisation unit, and are drawn by induced-draught fans to the chimney. (3) Feed water and steam circuit: water from the condenser is pumped through LP heaters, deaerator and HP heaters to the economiser and boiler drum (or once-through supercritical boiler); steam is superheated, expanded in HP turbine, reheated, and expanded through IP and LP turbines, then condensed in the surface condenser. (4) Cooling water circuit: cooling water from a river or cooling tower circulates through the condenser tubes. (5) The turbine drives the alternator, and power is stepped up by transformers to the grid. Overall efficiencies are about 33–38% for subcritical and up to 40–42% for supercritical and ultra-supercritical units.
Q: Compare reciprocating and rotary (centrifugal and axial) compressors.
Reciprocating compressors compress air by a piston in a cylinder in a positive-displacement manner. They give high pressure ratios (up to about 1000 bar with multistaging), but deliver small quantities of air intermittently, are heavy, need careful balancing and foundations, have many wearing parts (valves, rings) and the air may be contaminated with lubricating oil; their efficiency is good at high pressure ratios. Rotary positive-displacement compressors (vane, Roots, screw) give steady flow at moderate pressures. Dynamic (rotary) compressors – centrifugal and axial – add kinetic energy to the air by a rotating impeller or blades and convert it to pressure in diffusers. They handle large volume flows continuously and smoothly, are compact and light for their capacity, have few wearing parts, deliver oil-free air and can be directly coupled to turbines and motors at high speed; but the pressure ratio per stage is low (centrifugal about 4:1 per stage, axial about 1.2–1.3 per stage), and they suffer from surging and choking and lose efficiency away from the design point. Centrifugal compressors are used in turbochargers, small gas turbines and refrigeration chillers; axial compressors, with many stages and high efficiency, are used in large gas turbines and jet engines.

## P14. RAC and Heat Transfer
Q: Explain the working of a vapour absorption refrigeration system (aqua-ammonia) with its components.
In the aqua-ammonia system, ammonia is the refrigerant and water the absorbent. Low-pressure ammonia vapour from the evaporator enters the absorber, where it is absorbed by the weak aqua-ammonia solution; since absorption releases heat, the absorber is cooled by water to keep its capacity high. The resulting strong solution is pumped to the generator at condenser pressure, passing through a heat exchanger where it is preheated by the hot weak solution returning from the generator. In the generator, heat from steam, gas or waste heat drives ammonia vapour out of the strong solution. The vapour, which carries some water vapour, passes through an analyser (where it meets the incoming strong solution and some water condenses) and a rectifier (a water-cooled dephlegmator that condenses the remaining water and returns it to the generator), so nearly pure ammonia reaches the condenser. It is condensed, passes through the expansion valve and evaporates in the evaporator, producing refrigeration, and returns to the absorber. The weak solution from the generator returns to the absorber through the heat exchanger and a pressure-reducing valve. Since the pump handles liquid, its work is very small; the main energy input is heat. The system can use low-grade heat and has few moving parts, but its COP is low (0.5–0.7).
Q: Explain Kirchhoff's law of radiation and the concept of a grey body.
Consider a small body placed inside an isothermal enclosure and in thermal equilibrium with it. Radiation inside the enclosure is black-body radiation with irradiation G = σT⁴. The body absorbs αG and emits E; for equilibrium E = αG, so E/α = σT⁴ = E_b. Since this holds for any body, the ratio of emissive power to absorptivity is the same for all bodies at a given temperature and equals the emissive power of a black body. Defining emissivity ε = E/E_b gives ε = α: at thermal equilibrium the emissivity of a surface equals its absorptivity. The law holds strictly for spectral, directional properties; for total properties it holds when the incident radiation comes from a source at the same temperature as the surface. A grey body is an idealised surface whose spectral emissivity (and absorptivity) is the same at all wavelengths. For a grey body, the total emissivity equals the total absorptivity regardless of the source temperature, so Kirchhoff's law can be applied freely in engineering calculations of radiation exchange between real surfaces, which are approximated as grey and diffuse.
