# Paper-II Model Answers
Descriptive answers in the length expected for SSC JE Paper-II (conventional) – learn the structure: definition → explanation/sketch → equation → application.

## P1. Thermodynamics
Q: State the first law of thermodynamics for a closed system undergoing a cycle and a process, and show that internal energy is a property.
For a closed system executing a cycle, the net heat transfer equals the net work transfer: ∮δQ = ∮δW. For a process, the difference between heat supplied and work done appears as a change in stored energy: Q − W = ΔE (= ΔU when kinetic and potential energy changes are negligible). To show that U is a property, take a system from state 1 to state 2 by path A and return by path C; then take it from 1 to 2 by path B and return by path C. Applying the first law to both cycles: Q_A + Q_C = W_A + W_C and Q_B + Q_C = W_B + W_C. Subtracting, (Q − W)_A = (Q − W)_B. Since A and B are arbitrary, Q − W has the same value for every path between the same two states; it therefore depends only on the end states and is a point function – the change of a property called internal energy, dU = δQ − δW. Heat and work individually are path functions, but their difference is not.
Q: Derive the steady-flow energy equation and apply it to a turbine, nozzle and throttling valve.
Consider a control volume with one inlet and one outlet under steady conditions (mass flow, properties and energy content inside do not change with time). Energy entering per unit mass = h₁ + V₁²/2 + gz₁ (enthalpy includes flow work p₁v₁) plus heat q; energy leaving = h₂ + V₂²/2 + gz₂ plus shaft work w. Hence q − w = (h₂ − h₁) + (V₂² − V₁²)/2 + g(z₂ − z₁) per kg. Turbine: adiabatic, KE and PE changes small → w = h₁ − h₂, the work output equals the enthalpy drop. Nozzle: no work, adiabatic, PE negligible → V₂ = √(2(h₁ − h₂) + V₁²); with negligible inlet velocity V₂ = 44.72√(h₁ − h₂) with Δh in kJ/kg. Throttling valve: no work, adiabatic, negligible KE change → h₁ = h₂, so throttling is an isenthalpic, irreversible process with a pressure drop (temperature constant for an ideal gas, usually falls for real gases below the inversion temperature).
Q: Explain the Carnot cycle with p–v and T–s diagrams and prove that no engine can be more efficient than a reversible engine between the same reservoirs.
The Carnot cycle consists of four reversible processes: isothermal heat addition at T₁ (1–2), reversible adiabatic expansion from T₁ to T₂ (2–3), isothermal heat rejection at T₂ (3–4) and reversible adiabatic compression back to T₁ (4–1). On the T–s diagram it is a rectangle; heat supplied = T₁Δs, heat rejected = T₂Δs, so η = 1 − T₂/T₁. Carnot's theorem: suppose an engine E is more efficient than a reversible engine R working between the same reservoirs. Let R be reversed as a refrigerator driven by part of the work of E, with both handling the same heat from the hot reservoir. Since E is more efficient it produces more work than R needs, so the net effect is that the combination produces net work while exchanging heat only with the cold reservoir – a violation of the Kelvin–Planck statement. Hence no engine can be more efficient than a reversible one, and all reversible engines between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency, depending only on the temperatures. This leads to the thermodynamic (Kelvin) temperature scale.
Q: What is entropy? Explain the principle of increase of entropy.
Entropy is a property whose change in a reversible process is dS = δQ_rev/T; it measures molecular disorder and the degradation of energy. It follows from the Clausius inequality ∮δQ/T ≤ 0, which shows that ∫δQ_rev/T between two states is independent of path. For any process, dS ≥ δQ/T, the equality holding for reversible processes; the difference is entropy generation S_gen ≥ 0, caused by irreversibilities such as friction, heat transfer across finite temperature differences, unrestrained expansion and mixing. For an isolated system δQ = 0, so dS_isolated ≥ 0 – the entropy of an isolated system can never decrease. The universe (system + surroundings) is isolated, so every real process increases the entropy of the universe. This principle determines the direction of natural processes, gives the condition of equilibrium (maximum entropy) and quantifies lost work as T₀S_gen (Gouy–Stodola theorem).
Q: Define available energy, unavailable energy and irreversibility, and explain the loss of available energy due to heat transfer through a finite temperature difference.
Available energy (exergy) is the maximum useful work obtainable from a system or heat source as it comes to equilibrium with the surroundings at T₀. For heat Q at constant temperature T, AE = Q(1 − T₀/T); the rest, T₀Q/T = T₀ΔS, is unavailable energy that must be rejected to the surroundings. Irreversibility is the difference between the maximum (reversible) work and actual work, I = T₀S_gen. When heat Q flows from a body at T₁ to one at T₂ < T₁, its available energy falls from Q(1 − T₀/T₁) to Q(1 − T₀/T₂). The loss is T₀Q(1/T₂ − 1/T₁) = T₀ΔS_universe. So although energy is conserved, its quality is degraded; the larger the temperature difference, the larger the loss. This is why heat exchangers and boilers are designed to keep temperature differences small, and why regenerative feed heating improves steam-plant efficiency.

## P2. IC Engines
Q: Compare four-stroke and two-stroke engines.
A four-stroke engine completes its cycle in four strokes (suction, compression, power, exhaust) – two crankshaft revolutions – so there is one power stroke every two revolutions; a two-stroke engine completes the cycle in two strokes (one revolution) by using ports uncovered by the piston and crankcase or blower scavenging, giving one power stroke per revolution. Consequently the two-stroke engine theoretically develops twice the power for the same size and speed (actually 1.6–1.8 times), has a lighter flywheel and more uniform torque, a simpler mechanism (no valves or camshaft) and lower cost. However, scavenging losses (fresh charge escaping with exhaust) and incomplete exhaust give higher fuel consumption, higher HC emissions, lower thermal and volumetric efficiency, greater heating (more frequent power strokes) and higher lubricating-oil consumption (oil mixed with petrol in small engines). Four-stroke engines are used in cars, trucks and most motorcycles; two-stroke engines in small mopeds, chainsaws, outboard motors and very large marine diesel engines (where uniflow scavenging gives high efficiency).
Q: Explain detonation in SI engines and the factors affecting it.
Detonation (knock) in an SI engine is the spontaneous autoignition of the end gas – the last part of the unburnt charge – ahead of the flame front. As the flame advances from the spark plug, it compresses and heats the remaining charge; if that charge reaches its self-ignition temperature and its ignition delay ends before the flame arrives, it ignites almost instantaneously, producing a sharp pressure rise and pressure waves that cause the characteristic metallic pinging sound. Effects include noise, mechanical stress, overheating of the piston crown and spark plug, erosion, loss of power and efficiency, and in severe cases piston damage. Detonation is favoured by factors that raise the end-gas temperature and pressure or give it more time: high compression ratio, advanced spark timing, high intake temperature and pressure (supercharging), low engine speed, large bore (long flame travel), high wall temperature, lean or slightly rich mixtures at certain conditions and low-octane fuel. It is reduced by using high-octane fuel, lowering compression ratio, retarding the spark, increasing turbulence (shorter combustion time), central spark plug location, compact combustion chambers and good cooling.
Q: Describe the methods used to measure the frictional power of an engine.
(1) Willan's line method (CI engines): fuel consumption is plotted against brake power at constant speed; the nearly straight line is extrapolated back to zero fuel consumption, and the negative intercept on the BP axis gives the friction power. It is not suitable for SI engines because throttling makes the line non-linear. (2) Morse test (multi-cylinder engines): BP is measured with all cylinders firing, then with each cylinder cut out in turn at the same speed (by shorting the spark plug or cutting the fuel injector); the drop in BP equals the IP of the cut-out cylinder, and ΣIP − BP = FP. (3) Motoring test: the engine is run at the test speed by an electric dynamometer with no combustion; the power absorbed approximates FP, though it overestimates pumping losses at lower temperature. (4) Indicator diagram: IP is obtained from the indicated mean effective pressure and FP = IP − BP. (5) Retardation test: the engine is cut off and its deceleration measured to find friction torque.

## P3. Steam Power Plant
Q: Explain the Rankine cycle and the methods to improve its efficiency.
The Rankine cycle is the ideal cycle of a steam power plant: (1–2) isentropic expansion of steam in the turbine; (2–3) constant-pressure heat rejection in the condenser until the steam is saturated liquid; (3–4) isentropic pumping of water to boiler pressure; (4–1) constant-pressure heating in the boiler, through sensible heating, evaporation and superheating. Efficiency = (W_t − W_p)/Q₁ = [(h₁ − h₂) − (h₄ − h₃)]/(h₁ − h₄). Unlike Carnot, condensation is complete, so pumping a liquid needs very little work and the work ratio is close to 1. Efficiency is improved by raising the mean temperature of heat addition or lowering that of heat rejection: (a) lowering condenser pressure (limited by cooling-water temperature and turbine-exit wetness); (b) superheating, which increases both work and exit dryness; (c) increasing boiler pressure, which raises efficiency but reduces exit dryness – used with superheat and reheat; (d) reheating the steam after partial expansion, mainly to keep exit dryness above about 88%; (e) regenerative feed heating with bled steam, which raises feed-water temperature and the mean temperature of heat addition; (f) supercritical pressures, combined cycles and binary vapour cycles.
Q: Distinguish between fire-tube and water-tube boilers.
In fire-tube boilers hot flue gases flow inside tubes surrounded by water (Cochran, Lancashire, locomotive); in water-tube boilers water circulates inside tubes heated externally by gases (Babcock & Wilcox, Stirling, La Mont, Benson). Fire-tube boilers are limited to about 25 bar and modest capacities (up to about 9,000 kg/h) because the large shell must withstand the pressure; water-tube boilers handle high pressures (above 100 bar, even supercritical) and capacities of hundreds of tonnes per hour since the small tubes are strong. Water-tube boilers raise steam quickly due to small water content and good circulation, respond rapidly to load changes, and are safer because a tube burst releases little water; fire-tube boilers hold a large volume of water, so steam raising is slow but they cope with sudden demand from their stored energy. Fire-tube boilers are compact, cheaper, simple to operate and tolerate poorer water; water-tube boilers need treated water and skilled operation but are used in power stations, while fire-tube boilers suit small industries, process heating and locomotives.
Q: Explain the principle of compounding of steam turbines and its types.
If the whole enthalpy drop is expanded in one row of nozzles, steam velocity becomes very high (above 1,000 m/s); for good efficiency the blade speed must be about half this, leading to excessive rotor speeds (De Laval turbines ran up to 30,000 rpm), large centrifugal stresses and high carry-over losses. Compounding uses several rows of blades to absorb the energy in stages and reduce the rotor speed. (1) Pressure compounding (Rateau): the total pressure drop is divided among several stages, each consisting of a nozzle ring and a row of moving blades; each stage acts as a simple impulse turbine with moderate velocity. (2) Velocity compounding (Curtis): the pressure drops entirely in one set of nozzles, and the high-velocity steam passes through two or three rows of moving blades separated by fixed guide blades that redirect it; this gives large work per stage at low blade speed but lower efficiency. (3) Pressure–velocity compounding combines both – a Curtis stage at the high-pressure end followed by Rateau stages. Reaction turbines (Parsons) are inherently pressure compounded because pressure drops in both fixed and moving blades in every stage.

## P4. Compressors and Gas Turbines
Q: Explain multistage compression with intercooling and its advantages.
In multistage compression air is compressed in two or more cylinders in series, and is cooled at constant pressure in an intercooler between stages. With perfect intercooling the air returns to its initial temperature before each stage, so the overall process approaches isothermal compression, which requires the minimum work. On the p–V diagram the shaded area between the single-stage polytropic curve and the stepped multistage curve represents the work saved. For minimum work with perfect intercooling the pressure ratio per stage is equal, p₂/p₁ = p₃/p₂ = (p_final/p₁)^(1/N), and each stage does equal work. Advantages: reduced total work and power; lower delivery temperatures, which help lubrication and reduce the risk of oil ignition; higher volumetric efficiency since each stage has a small pressure ratio and the effect of clearance is reduced; lighter cylinders for high-pressure stages; better mechanical balance and more uniform torque; moisture can be removed in intercoolers. Disadvantages are higher initial cost and complexity.
Q: Explain the methods to improve the efficiency and work output of a simple gas turbine plant.
The simple open-cycle gas turbine has low efficiency (around 20–30%) because the compressor consumes a large part of the turbine work and hot exhaust is discharged. (1) Regeneration: exhaust gases heat the compressed air in a heat exchanger before the combustion chamber, reducing fuel consumption; it is effective only when exhaust temperature exceeds compressor delivery temperature, i.e. at low pressure ratios. (2) Intercooling between compressor stages reduces compressor work and increases net work, though it lowers the temperature at which heat is added; with regeneration it improves efficiency. (3) Reheating between turbine stages raises turbine work; combined with regeneration it increases efficiency. (4) Increasing turbine inlet temperature through better materials (nickel superalloys, ceramic coatings) and blade cooling. (5) Improving compressor and turbine efficiencies. (6) Combined cycles: the 500–600 °C exhaust raises steam in a heat-recovery steam generator for a steam turbine, giving overall efficiencies above 55–60%.

## P5. Refrigeration and Air Conditioning
Q: Explain a simple vapour compression refrigeration system with p–h and T–s diagrams, and the effect of subcooling and superheating.
A VCR system has four components. Low-pressure dry saturated vapour from the evaporator is compressed isentropically (1–2) to the condenser pressure, becoming superheated. In the condenser (2–3) it rejects heat to cooling air or water at constant pressure, desuperheating and condensing to saturated liquid. The liquid is throttled in the expansion valve (3–4) at constant enthalpy to evaporator pressure, partly flashing to vapour. In the evaporator (4–1) the liquid boils at low temperature, absorbing heat from the space. Refrigerating effect = h₁ − h₄, compressor work = h₂ − h₁, COP = (h₁ − h₄)/(h₂ − h₁). On the p–h chart the cycle is a near-rectangle with the expansion as a vertical line. Subcooling the condensate below saturation moves point 3 to the left, lowering h₄; the refrigerating effect increases without extra work, so COP rises – achieved with extra condenser surface or a liquid–suction heat exchanger. Superheating the vapour at the evaporator exit, if it is done in the refrigerated space, increases the refrigerating effect, but compressor work and specific volume also increase; the effect on COP depends on the refrigerant. Superheating is still useful because it prevents liquid from entering the compressor.
Q: Explain the psychrometric processes involved in winter air conditioning.
In winter the outdoor air is cold and, although its relative humidity may be high, its specific humidity is low; heating it without adding moisture would give very dry air. Winter air conditioning therefore involves heating with humidification. Fresh and recirculated air are mixed and filtered, then heated in a preheater coil (sensible heating, horizontal line on the chart). The air then passes through a humidifier – a spray of water (adiabatic, along the WBT line, which cools the air and raises its humidity) or steam injection (nearly vertical, slight temperature rise). Finally a reheater coil raises the temperature to the required supply condition (sensible heating). The conditioned air, slightly warmer than the room, is supplied to offset heat loss through walls and infiltration, maintaining about 20–22 °C and 40–50% RH. Controls include a humidistat for the humidifier and thermostats for the heating coils.

## P6. Heat Transfer
Q: Define fin efficiency and fin effectiveness. When are fins justified?
Fins (extended surfaces) increase the area for convective heat transfer. Fin efficiency is the ratio of the actual heat dissipated by the fin to the heat it would dissipate if its entire surface were at the base temperature; because temperature falls along the fin, efficiency is less than one. For a fin with an insulated tip η_f = tanh(mL)/(mL), where m = √(hP/(kA_c)). Fin effectiveness is the ratio of heat transfer with the fin to that from the base area without the fin; for a long fin ε = √(kP/(hA_c)). A fin is worthwhile only if effectiveness is well above unity – a practical criterion is ε > 2. High effectiveness requires high thermal conductivity, a large perimeter-to-area ratio (thin fins) and a low heat-transfer coefficient; therefore fins are used on the gas or air side of heat exchangers (car radiators, air-cooled engine cylinders, condensers, electronic heat sinks) and not where h is already high (boiling or condensing). Increasing the length beyond mL ≈ 3 gives little benefit.
Q: Derive the expression for LMTD of a parallel-flow heat exchanger.
Consider an element of area dA where the hot fluid temperature is T_h and the cold T_c, with local difference θ = T_h − T_c. The heat transferred is dQ = U dA θ. The hot fluid cools: dQ = −C_h dT_h; the cold fluid heats: dQ = C_c dT_c. Hence dθ = dT_h − dT_c = −dQ(1/C_h + 1/C_c). Substituting dQ = U dA θ: dθ/θ = −U(1/C_h + 1/C_c) dA. Integrating from inlet (θ₁) to outlet (θ₂): ln(θ₂/θ₁) = −UA(1/C_h + 1/C_c). Over the whole exchanger Q = C_h(T_h1 − T_h2) = C_c(T_c2 − T_c1), so 1/C_h + 1/C_c = [(T_h1 − T_h2) + (T_c2 − T_c1)]/Q = (θ₁ − θ₂)/Q. Therefore ln(θ₁/θ₂) = UA(θ₁ − θ₂)/Q, or Q = UA(θ₁ − θ₂)/ln(θ₁/θ₂) = UA × LMTD. For parallel flow θ₁ = T_h1 − T_c1 and θ₂ = T_h2 − T_c2. The same form applies to counter flow with θ₁ = T_h1 − T_c2 and θ₂ = T_h2 − T_c1.
Q: Explain the regimes of pool boiling with a boiling curve.
When a heated surface is immersed in a pool of saturated liquid, the heat flux varies with the excess temperature ΔT_e = T_s − T_sat as shown by Nukiyama's boiling curve. (1) Natural-convection boiling (ΔT_e below about 5 °C for water): the liquid is slightly superheated and moves by free convection; evaporation occurs at the free surface. (2) Nucleate boiling (about 5–30 °C): bubbles form at nucleation sites (cavities) on the surface; at first they are isolated and collapse in the liquid, then they form jets and columns. Intense agitation gives very high heat-transfer coefficients, and heat flux rises steeply to a maximum – the critical (peak) heat flux, about 1 MW/m² for water at atmospheric pressure. (3) Transition boiling (about 30–120 °C): an unstable vapour film partly covers the surface; because vapour conducts poorly, heat flux decreases as ΔT_e rises, down to the minimum heat flux at the Leidenfrost point. (4) Film boiling (above about 120 °C): a stable vapour film blankets the surface; heat passes by conduction and radiation through the film, and flux rises again only at very high temperatures. In heat-flux-controlled equipment, exceeding the critical flux causes a jump from nucleate to film boiling with a huge temperature rise (burnout), so boilers and nuclear reactors are designed to operate in nucleate boiling with a safety margin below CHF.
