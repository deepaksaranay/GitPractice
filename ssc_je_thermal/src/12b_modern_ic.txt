# IC Engines – Modern Technologies, Emission Norms and Special Engines

## 12B.1 Fuel Injection and Engine Management
| System | Description | Benefit |
| Throttle-body injection (TBI, single point) | One or two injectors above the throttle | Better metering than carburettor; simple |
| Multi-point fuel injection (MPFI, port injection) | One injector per cylinder spraying at the intake port; timed sequentially by ECU | Uniform distribution, better economy and emissions, quick response |
| Gasoline direct injection (GDI) | Fuel injected directly into the cylinder at 50–200 bar; homogeneous or stratified charge | Charge cooling allows higher r, better economy; but more particulate emissions |
| Common rail direct injection (CRDI) | High-pressure pump keeps fuel in a common rail at ≈ 1,600–2,500 bar; ECU-controlled solenoid or piezo injectors | Precise timing, multiple injections (pilot, main, post) – quieter combustion, lower NOx and smoke, better economy |
| Unit injector | Pump and injector combined for each cylinder, cam-driven | Very high pressure, used in heavy engines |
- The engine control unit (ECU) reads sensors and controls injection quantity and timing, ignition timing, idle speed, EGR and turbo boost.
| Sensor | Function |
| MAF (mass air flow, hot-wire/hot-film) / MAP (manifold absolute pressure) | Air quantity for fuel calculation |
| Oxygen (lambda) sensor (zirconia) | Exhaust O₂ – closed-loop control to keep λ ≈ 1 for the three-way catalyst |
| Throttle position sensor | Driver demand, acceleration enrichment |
| Crankshaft/camshaft position sensors | Engine speed and piston position for injection and ignition timing |
| Coolant temperature sensor | Cold-start enrichment, fan control |
| Knock sensor (piezoelectric) | Detects detonation vibrations; ECU retards spark |
- Ignition: conventional battery/coil with contact breaker → electronic (transistorised) ignition → distributor-less ignition (DIS) → coil-on-plug; magneto ignition in two-wheelers and small engines without battery.
- Variable valve timing (VVT) and lift (e.g. VTEC, VVT-i): adjusts valve events with speed and load for better torque, power and economy. Cylinder deactivation shuts off cylinders at light load.
- Downsizing: smaller turbocharged GDI engines replace larger naturally aspirated engines with equal power and lower fuel use; intercooler cools the compressed air.
PYQ-TREND: MPFI vs carburettor; CRDI common rail pressure and multiple injections (very frequent); lambda sensor for closed-loop control; knock sensor piezoelectric; GDI.

## 12B.2 Emission Control Technologies
| Device | Purpose |
| Three-way catalytic converter (Pt, Pd, Rh on ceramic honeycomb) | Oxidises CO and HC, reduces NOx – effective only near stoichiometric (λ ≈ 1) in SI engines |
| Diesel oxidation catalyst (DOC) | Oxidises CO, HC and soluble organic part of PM in diesel exhaust |
| Diesel particulate filter (DPF) | Wall-flow ceramic filter traps soot; periodically regenerated by burning the soot |
| Selective catalytic reduction (SCR) | Urea solution (AdBlue/DEF) injected, forms NH₃ which reduces NOx to N₂ and H₂O |
| Lean NOx trap (LNT) | Stores NOx during lean operation, releases and reduces it during brief rich periods |
| Exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) | Recirculated exhaust lowers peak combustion temperature – reduces NOx |
| Positive crankcase ventilation (PCV) | Blow-by gases returned to the intake – reduces HC |
| Evaporative emission control (charcoal canister) | Traps fuel vapour from the tank |
| On-board diagnostics (OBD) | Monitors emission-related components and warns the driver |
- Trade-off in diesel engines: measures that reduce NOx (lower combustion temperature, retarded injection) tend to increase PM and fuel consumption, and vice versa – the 'NOx–PM trade-off'; after-treatment (DPF + SCR) breaks it.
- Indian norms (Bharat Stage, based on Euro norms): BS-IV nationwide from April 2017; India leapfrogged BS-V and implemented BS-VI from 1 April 2020 with 10 ppm sulphur fuel; BS-VI Phase 2 (2023) added real driving emission (RDE) testing and stricter OBD.
- Alternative fuels and blends: ethanol blending (E20 petrol targeted for 2025), flex-fuel vehicles, CNG (high octane, low PM), LPG, biodiesel blends (B20), hydrogen ICE and fuel-cell vehicles.
PYQ-TREND: Three-way catalyst needs stoichiometric mixture; SCR uses urea (very frequent); DPF traps soot; EGR reduces NOx; BS-VI from 2020 skipping BS-V; NOx–PM trade-off; PCV and charcoal canister.

## 12B.3 Hybrid and Advanced Combustion Concepts
| Concept | Description |
| Series hybrid | Engine drives only a generator; wheels driven by electric motor |
| Parallel hybrid | Engine and motor can both drive the wheels |
| Series–parallel (power-split) hybrid | Planetary gear splits engine power between wheels and generator (e.g. Toyota hybrid system) |
| Mild hybrid / start–stop | Small motor-generator assists the engine, stops it at idle and recovers braking energy |
| Plug-in hybrid (PHEV) | Larger battery charged from the grid; electric-only range |
| HCCI (homogeneous charge compression ignition) | Premixed lean charge autoignites by compression – very low NOx and soot, high efficiency, but difficult to control combustion timing |
| Stratified-charge engine | Rich mixture near the spark plug, lean elsewhere – overall lean operation |
| Dual-fuel engine | Gaseous fuel (CNG/biogas) premixed with air, ignited by a pilot diesel injection |
- Regenerative braking converts kinetic energy into electrical energy stored in the battery, improving city fuel economy.

## 12B.4 Special Engines
| Engine | Key features |
| Wankel (rotary) engine | Triangular rotor in an epitrochoidal housing; three power impulses per rotor revolution (one per output-shaft revolution); compact, light, smooth, few parts; drawbacks – apex-seal wear and leakage, high HC emissions and fuel consumption, low compression ratio |
| Stirling engine | External combustion, closed regenerative cycle with working gas (air, He, H₂); quiet, multi-fuel (solar, biomass), theoretically Carnot efficiency; low power density, high cost |
| Free-piston engine | No crankshaft; pistons drive a compressor or linear generator |
| Gas engines | Run on natural gas, biogas, producer gas; spark ignited or dual fuel |
| Opposed-piston engine | Two pistons per cylinder moving toward each other; no cylinder head; used in some two-stroke diesels |
| Variable compression ratio engine | Changes r with load for efficiency and knock control |
PYQ-TREND: Wankel engine rotor shape and apex seals (frequent); Stirling engine is external combustion with regenerator; dual-fuel engine with pilot diesel.

## 12B.5 Engine Balancing and Flywheel (Quick Facts)
- Primary unbalanced force of a reciprocating mass: F_p = m r ω² cosθ; secondary force: F_s = m r ω² cos2θ/n (n = l/r, ratio of connecting rod length to crank radius). Secondary force varies at twice crank speed.
- Single-cylinder engines are partially balanced by rotating counterweights (balancing usually 2/3 to 3/4 of reciprocating mass), which introduces an unbalanced force perpendicular to the line of stroke.
- In-line four-cylinder engine (cranks at 180°) is balanced for primary forces and couples but has unbalanced secondary forces; six-cylinder in-line engine is fully balanced for primary and secondary forces and couples.
- Flywheel stores energy during the power stroke and releases it during other strokes, reducing speed fluctuation. Coefficient of fluctuation of speed C_s = (N_max − N_min)/N_mean; energy fluctuation ΔE = I ω² C_s. Two-stroke and multi-cylinder engines need smaller flywheels than single-cylinder four-stroke engines because torque is more uniform.
F: ΔE = I ω² C_s = m k² ω² C_s;  C_s = (ω_max − ω_min)/ω_mean
PYQ-TREND: Six-cylinder in-line engine fully balanced; four-stroke single-cylinder engine needs the heaviest flywheel; secondary force at twice crank speed.
Q: Explain the working of a common rail direct injection (CRDI) system and its advantages.
In a CRDI system a high-pressure pump, driven by the engine, supplies diesel continuously to a thick-walled common rail (accumulator) that is kept at a very high, controlled pressure – typically 1,600 to 2,500 bar – independent of engine speed. Short high-pressure pipes connect the rail to electronically controlled injectors (solenoid or piezoelectric) in each cylinder. The ECU, using inputs from crank-position, accelerator, air-flow, rail-pressure and temperature sensors, decides when and for how long each injector opens, so the injection timing, quantity and pressure are precisely controlled. It can split injection into pilot, main and post injections: the small pilot injection shortens the ignition delay of the main injection, reducing diesel knock and noise; post injections help burn soot and regenerate after-treatment devices. Advantages: finer atomisation and better mixing due to high pressure, lower smoke and NOx, better fuel economy, higher power and torque, quieter and smoother running, and flexibility to meet BS-VI norms with after-treatment. Disadvantages include high cost, sensitivity to fuel quality and the need for clean fuel filtration and electronic diagnostics.
