# Paper-II Model Answers

## P1. Statics and Kinematics
Q: Explain the different types of manometers with their uses and limitations.
A manometer measures pressure by balancing it against a column of liquid. (1) Piezometer – a vertical tube open to air; simplest, measures moderate positive gauge pressure of liquids only; cannot measure vacuum, gas pressure or high pressures (column too tall). (2) Simple U-tube – one limb connected to the pipe, the other open, containing a heavier immiscible liquid (mercury); measures positive or negative gauge pressure of liquids and gases. (3) Single-column (well-type) manometer – one limb replaced by a large reservoir, so only one level change needs reading; the inclined version magnifies small readings (L = h/sinθ) for low pressures such as air ducts. (4) Differential U-tube – both limbs connected to two points; gives the pressure difference, e.g., across a venturimeter: p_A − p_B = gx(ρm − ρ). (5) Inverted U-tube – a lighter fluid (air/oil) at the top; for small pressure differences between two points in a liquid. (6) Micromanometers for very small differences. Limitations: slow response, unsuitable for fluctuating pressures, need careful reading, capillary error in narrow tubes, mercury toxicity.
Q: Derive the continuity equation in Cartesian coordinates.
Consider a fluid element of sides dx, dy, dz. Mass flux entering the face normal to x = ρu dy dz; leaving = [ρu + ∂(ρu)/∂x dx] dy dz; net outflow in x = ∂(ρu)/∂x dx dy dz. Similarly for y and z. The rate of decrease of mass inside = −∂ρ/∂t dx dy dz. By conservation of mass, net outflow = rate of decrease of mass: ∂ρ/∂t + ∂(ρu)/∂x + ∂(ρv)/∂y + ∂(ρw)/∂z = 0. For steady flow ∂ρ/∂t = 0; for incompressible flow ρ is constant and the equation reduces to ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y + ∂w/∂z = 0. In one-dimensional pipe flow it gives ρ₁A₁V₁ = ρ₂A₂V₂ (or A₁V₁ = A₂V₂ for liquids).
Q: Explain free and forced vortex flows with examples, and derive the equation of the free surface in a forced vortex.
In a vortex the fluid moves along curved paths. In a forced vortex the fluid rotates like a rigid body under an external torque, with V = ωr; the flow is rotational and energy is supplied continuously – e.g., liquid in a rotating vessel, flow inside a centrifugal pump impeller or turbine runner. In a free vortex no external torque acts, angular momentum is conserved and Vr = constant; the flow is irrotational – e.g., whirlpool, flow into a sink drain, flow in a pump casing outside the impeller. For a forced vortex, consider pressure variation: ∂p/∂r = ρV²/r = ρω²r and ∂p/∂z = −ρg. Integrating, p = ρω²r²/2 − ρgz + C. On the free surface p = atmospheric (constant), so z = ω²r²/(2g) + constant: the free surface is a paraboloid of revolution. The height of the paraboloid for radius R is h = ω²R²/(2g); because a paraboloid's volume is half its circumscribing cylinder, the liquid rises h/2 at the wall and falls h/2 at the centre from the original level.

## P2. Dynamics and Pipe Flow
Q: Derive the expression for the force exerted by a flowing fluid on a pipe bend.
Take a control volume of fluid in the bend between sections 1 (area A₁, pressure p₁, velocity V₁ along x) and 2 (A₂, p₂, V₂ inclined at θ to x). Forces on the fluid: pressure forces p₁A₁ (in +x) and p₂A₂ (opposite to V₂), and the reaction of the bend (−F_x, −F_y). Applying the momentum equation in x: p₁A₁ − p₂A₂ cosθ − F_x = ρQ(V₂ cosθ − V₁), so F_x = ρQ(V₁ − V₂ cosθ) + p₁A₁ − p₂A₂ cosθ. In y: −p₂A₂ sinθ − F_y = ρQ(V₂ sinθ − 0), so F_y = −ρQV₂ sinθ − p₂A₂ sinθ (i.e., magnitude ρQV₂ sinθ + p₂A₂ sinθ in the −y direction). The resultant F = √(F_x² + F_y²) at tan⁻¹(F_y/F_x). Anchor blocks are designed to resist this force.
Q: What is the Moody diagram? Explain its zones.
The Moody diagram is a log–log chart of the Darcy friction factor f against Reynolds number for different relative roughness ε/D, based on the Colebrook–White equation. Laminar zone (Re < 2000): f = 64/Re, a single straight line independent of roughness. Critical zone (2000–4000): flow is unstable and f uncertain. Transition (turbulent) zone: f depends on both Re and ε/D; the smooth-pipe curve is the lower limit (Blasius f = 0.316/Re^0.25 for Re < 10⁵). Fully rough zone (high Re, to the right of a dashed boundary): curves become horizontal and f depends only on ε/D, because roughness elements project through the laminar sub-layer and losses are due to form drag on them; head loss then varies as V². The diagram is used to find f for Darcy–Weisbach calculations and to solve pipe-sizing problems.
Q: Derive the condition for maximum power transmission through a pipe.
Water from a reservoir of head H flows through a pipe of length L and diameter D. Head at the outlet = H − h_f, with h_f = fLV²/(2gD). Power transmitted P = ρgQ(H − h_f) = ρg(πD²/4)V(H − fLV²/(2gD)). For a given pipe, P is maximum when dP/dV = 0: H − 3fLV²/(2gD) = 0, i.e., h_f = H/3. The efficiency of transmission η = (H − h_f)/H then equals 2/3 or 66.67%. In hydro power plants the penstock is designed for lower losses than this (higher efficiency), but the result explains why friction must be limited.

## P3. Machines
Q: Explain the working of a Kaplan turbine and state why it is suitable for low heads.
A Kaplan turbine is an axial-flow reaction turbine. Water from the scroll casing passes through guide vanes (wicket gates), turns through 90° in the whirl space and flows axially through a runner shaped like a ship's propeller with 3–8 blades on a large hub. The runner blades are twisted and can be rotated about their own axes by a servomotor in the hub, together with the guide vanes ('double regulation'), so the blade angles match the flow at every load. Water then leaves through an elbow draft tube. At low heads (2–70 m) a large discharge is needed for appreciable power; the axial runner passes large flows through a small diameter at high rotational speed, giving a high specific speed (255–860), which allows a compact, high-speed generator. Adjustable blades keep efficiency high (≈ 90%) over a wide range of load, unlike a fixed-blade propeller turbine.
Q: Draw and explain the characteristic curves of a centrifugal pump.
Main characteristic curves are drawn at constant speed with discharge Q on the x-axis. Head H: maximum at zero flow (shut-off head) and falling with Q for backward-curved vanes, because of increasing losses and slip. Shaft power P: rises gradually with Q; for backward-curved vanes it levels off (non-overloading), so the motor cannot be overloaded at high flows. Efficiency η: zero at Q = 0, rising to a maximum at the design (best efficiency) point, then falling. The intersection of the H–Q curve with the system curve H = H_static + kQ² gives the operating point. Operating characteristic curves are the main curves at the rated speed; iso-efficiency (Muschel) curves join points of equal efficiency on H–Q plots at different speeds and help choose the best speed. Pumps are selected so that the operating point lies near the BEP.
Q: Explain the working of a reciprocating pump with an indicator diagram.
A single-acting reciprocating pump has a piston moving in a cylinder with a suction valve and a delivery valve, driven by a crank. During the suction stroke the piston moves away, pressure in the cylinder falls below atmospheric, the suction valve opens and liquid enters. During the delivery stroke the piston pushes the liquid out through the delivery valve. The ideal indicator diagram (pressure head vs stroke) is a rectangle: the suction line lies H_atm − h_s and the delivery line H_atm + h_d, so its area ∝ ρgALN(h_s + h_d)/60, the work done. Acceleration of the water columns (h_a = (L/g)(A/a)ω²r cosθ) tilts these lines – pressure is lowest at the start of suction and highest at the start of delivery – but does not change the area. Pipe friction (maximum at mid-stroke) bulges the lines parabolically and increases the area (more work). Air vessels near the cylinder reduce both effects, smooth the flow and allow higher speeds without separation.
Q: Compare centrifugal and reciprocating pumps.
Centrifugal pumps give a continuous, smooth discharge, handle large flows at low to medium heads, run at high speeds with direct motor coupling, are compact, cheap and simple to maintain, can handle dirty liquids, slurries and viscous liquids (with suitable impellers), but need priming and lose efficiency away from the design point. Reciprocating pumps give a small, pulsating discharge at high heads, are self-priming, maintain efficiency over a range of heads, but are bulky, costly, have many wearing parts (valves, packing), run at low speeds, need air vessels to smooth flow and are unsuitable for liquids with suspended solids. Hence centrifugal pumps dominate water supply, irrigation and process industries, while reciprocating pumps are used for high-pressure duties such as hydraulic testing, oil-well and boiler-feed service in small plants, and metering.
